## Question

How to choose between large and small models?

## Brief answer

Choose the smallest model that meets measured quality and operational requirements, and compare total serving cost rather than parameter count alone.

## Detailed answer

Larger models can handle more complex tasks but typically demand more memory and compute. A smaller specialist, retrieval pipeline, or routed system may meet the target more efficiently. Evaluate realistic input/output lengths, concurrency, hardware, and failure rates. Include retries, tool calls, and review costs: a cheap model that needs repeated correction may be more expensive per successful task.

Estimate cost per successful task rather than cost per token in isolation. A small model may generate cheap tokens but require longer prompts, multiple retries, extra verification, or human correction. A larger model can win economically if it succeeds more often or finishes with fewer steps. Conversely, a specialist classifier or adapted small model can be far cheaper when the task is narrow and well defined.

Benchmark a representative workload with realistic input lengths, output lengths, concurrency, and latency targets. Report quality by difficulty slice and measure the tail of latency, not just the average. Include memory for KV caches and runtime buffers, because parameter weight size alone does not determine the number of concurrent requests.

Routing can combine strengths: use a small model for routine extraction and escalate uncertain or complex cases to a stronger one. The router itself needs evaluation and an explicit fallback; confidence scores are not automatically calibrated. Select the simplest system that satisfies quality, privacy, and operating requirements, then revisit the choice when workload volume or failure costs change.
