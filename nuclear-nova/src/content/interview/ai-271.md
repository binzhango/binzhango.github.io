## Question

How do you build a training dataset without triggering model collapse when much of your data is synthetic?

## Brief answer

Keep an independently collected real-data anchor, track synthetic lineage, and select synthetic examples for verified correctness and coverage rather than volume. Collapse is a distributional problem: repeatedly learning from a model's own narrow outputs can erase rare modes and amplify errors. Synthetic data is useful when it adds trustworthy supervision rather than replacing the underlying distribution.

## Detailed answer

Start with a held-out, human-origin evaluation set split by source and time. Preserve minority languages, rare tasks, difficult examples, and negative cases in the training mixture. Generate multiple candidate answers from diverse prompts or teachers, then validate with execution, reference documents, or human review. A second model's agreement is weak evidence when both models share the same blind spots.

Deduplicate across generations and record teacher version, prompt, verification method, and ancestors. Cap repetitive synthetic families and retain fresh real observations. Compare mixture ratios in controlled training runs using tail-task accuracy, calibration, diversity, and held-out perplexity; do not choose solely on average loss. Reintroduce missing modes when a slice deteriorates. There is no universal safe synthetic percentage: the generation and selection process matter.

### Why the tail disappears

Consider a real distribution with 99% common examples and 1% rare examples. A teacher that produces only 0.2% rare examples makes that behavior even less visible to its student. Repeating generation, filtering, and replacement can eliminate the rare mode entirely. A filter that keeps only high-confidence, short answers can accelerate this loss even when every retained answer is individually correct.

For a coding dataset, generate variations of real tasks, execute candidate solutions against independent tests, and retain difficult failing cases as problems to solve rather than silently dropping them. Keep original task identities so ten paraphrases count as one family during sampling and train/test splitting. Run a real-only baseline and several synthetic-mixture experiments with identical token budgets. Accept a mixture only when its useful gains survive independent evaluation and its rare-task regressions are understood. Synthetic data can expand coverage; recursive replacement without fresh evidence is the risky mechanism.
