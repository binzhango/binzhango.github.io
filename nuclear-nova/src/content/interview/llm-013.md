## Question

Explain bias-variance tradeoff. How does it manifest in LLMs?

## Brief answer

Bias measures systematic error across possible training sets; variance measures how much predictions change with the training sample. Both help explain generalization, but LLM scaling does not follow a simple “larger means more overfitting” rule.

## Detailed answer

For squared-error regression, expected prediction error decomposes into squared bias, variance, and irreducible noise. Cross-entropy language modeling does not use that identical decomposition. In LLM fine-tuning, limited or repetitive data can encourage memorization, while insufficient capacity or optimization can cause underfitting. More parameters can improve generalization when accompanied by adequate data and training. Hallucination is not a synonym for high variance or overfitting, and a single pretraining epoch can still contain trillions of tokens.

For a fixed input $x$, imagine repeatedly drawing a training dataset and refitting the same learning procedure. Bias compares its average prediction with the true regression function. Variance measures how much the fitted predictions fluctuate around that average. The noise term represents variation in the outcome that the available inputs cannot predict. A model can have low training error and still high variance because its solution depends strongly on accidental training examples.

A simple polynomial illustrates the idea: a straight line may miss a curved relationship systematically, whereas a very flexible curve may follow each noisy observation. Regularization can reduce sensitivity at the cost of imposing additional structure. This intuition is useful, but the familiar U-shaped curve is not a universal law for overparameterized networks.

For an LLM adaptation, compare multiple data samples or seeds, train/holdout loss, and task-level results. An instruction model that memorizes a small set of long answers may fail paraphrases despite low training loss. That is evidence about generalization; a factual error alone does not identify which statistical component caused it.
