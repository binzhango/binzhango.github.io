## Question

You have hundreds of millions of fleet miles. How do you find and use the rare scenarios that matter?

## Brief answer

Use a scenario-mining pipeline that combines metadata rules, learned embeddings, uncertainty, interventions, and novelty to locate rare relevant events. Preserve sampling weights and keep an independent representative evaluation set. Training only on dramatic failures can distort the policy away from normal driving, while random sampling wastes most review effort.

## Detailed answer

**Suggested approach.** Segment fleet logs into temporally coherent windows with map, weather, actor, and maneuver metadata. Run inexpensive detectors to propose events, then cluster and review candidates for actual novelty and severity. Deduplicate near-identical drives and hold out related segments across train/test boundaries.

Prioritize cases that reveal a model weakness or safety-case gap, then produce labels and targeted simulation variants. Track discovery yield, label quality, downstream regression, and coverage of rare conditions. Use active learning carefully: uncertainty can reflect sensor corruption rather than useful behavior. Document the difference between targeted training frequency and real-world exposure frequency when estimating risk or reporting improvements.

### Build a funnel from cheap signals to useful training cases

Start with inexpensive triggers such as hard braking, unusual actor counts, construction metadata, interventions, and model disagreement. Retrieve candidate windows with enough pre-event and post-event context to understand what happened. Cluster them so thousands of nearly identical logs do not consume the review budget, then prioritize distinct high-value scenarios.

Human review separates meaningful edge cases from sensor corruption and labeling artifacts. A high-uncertainty frame may contain a dirty lens rather than a novel traffic interaction; both matter, but they call for different responses. Route the former toward fault handling and the latter toward behavior-data coverage.

Keep entire related drives or scenario families together when splitting data. Adjacent windows from the same incident in training and evaluation can create apparent generalization without testing it. Preserve an independent representative panel alongside targeted stress cases.

Use sampling weights or a separately designed exposure study when estimating fleet rates. A training set intentionally enriched with rare events cannot directly establish how often those events occur in deployment. After adding targeted data, evaluate both the intended improvement and regressions on ordinary driving. The pipeline succeeds when discovered scenarios lead to verified behavioral gains or stronger operating limits, not merely when it produces a large searchable archive.
