## Question

Your annotators have no ground truth: the tasks are subjective preference judgments. How do you measure and improve label quality?

## Brief answer

Measure consistency, calibration to an explicit rubric, and useful disagreement rather than treating majority preference as truth. Collect overlapping blinded judgments, repeat a small set of items, and separate ambiguity in the task from unreliable annotation. Improve the rubric and task presentation before excluding annotators for disagreement.

## Detailed answer

For each pair, record preferred response, confidence, and the reason tied to criteria such as factuality, relevance, and tone. Estimate pairwise agreement or Krippendorff's alpha with uncertainty, stratified by domain; low prevalence or many near-ties can complicate interpretation. Within-annotator repeated-item consistency helps distinguish individual noise from legitimate differences between groups.

Use expert discussion on disagreement clusters to refine examples and clarify incompatible objectives. A rater who consistently prefers concise answers may be following a different implicit criterion, not cheating. Preserve ties or preference distributions when warranted. Audit positional and demographic bias and compare downstream model performance using independently collected evaluation data. Compensation should not reward speed alone or agreement with a hidden majority, both of which can discourage careful judgment.

### Separate three sources of disagreement

First, two careful raters may disagree because the rubric trades concision against completeness without specifying priority. Second, an item may contain an objectively checkable factual error that one rater missed. Third, a worker may be inattentive or random. These require different remedies: clarify the rubric, improve evidence access or domain training, and investigate reliability respectively. A single agreement score cannot identify which problem dominates.

For an illustrative pair where answer A is short but omits a material caveat and answer B is longer but accurate, ask raters to score factuality and relevance separately before giving an overall preference. If they agree on those dimensions but disagree overall, the aggregation policy is the issue. If the same rater changes choices when answer order is swapped, investigate position bias with repeated blinded items.

Reserve an independent sample for measuring whether rubric changes actually improve consistency. Repeatedly training against the very items used to score workers can create agreement through memorization. Preserve meaningful minority preferences when the product serves different audiences; disagreement can be information about the target population rather than noise to eliminate.
