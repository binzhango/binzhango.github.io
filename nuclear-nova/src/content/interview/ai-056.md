## Question

Walk me through RLHF end to end: reward model, policy optimisation, KL penalty.

## Brief answer

A common RLHF pipeline first trains a policy with demonstrations, then fits a reward model to preference comparisons and optimizes the policy against that reward while limiting departure from a reference policy.

## Detailed answer

For a prompt, collect candidate responses and human preferences. Train the reward model to rank preferred responses, then use policy optimization such as PPO with a reward and KL regularization. The KL term discourages exploiting the reward model through extreme distribution shift, but does not prevent all reward hacking. Audit annotator disagreement, reward-model generalization and downstream behavior.

A common reward model takes a prompt-response pair and produces a scalar $r(x,y)$. Given a preferred response $y_w$ and rejected response $y_l$, a pairwise objective encourages $r(x,y_w)>r(x,y_l)$, often through a logistic loss on their difference. The score is a learned preference proxy, not a calibrated probability that an answer is correct.

Policy optimization samples new responses and improves a reward such as $r(x,y)-\beta\log[\pi(y\mid x)/\pi_{ref}(y\mid x)]$. The reference term discourages moving too far from a useful starting policy. PPO additionally limits the size of policy updates through a clipped surrogate objective and commonly uses a learned value function to estimate advantages. Rollout generation, reward scoring, policy updates and value updates all consume resources.

The data loop matters as much as the optimizer. Use diverse prompts, clear annotation rubrics, disagreement review and held-out preference tests. If annotators consistently favor longer responses, the reward model may learn length as a shortcut. As optimization progresses, the policy can exploit weaknesses in the reward model, so compare against independent human and executable evaluations. Track helpfulness, factuality, refusal behavior and general capabilities; a rising training reward alone is not sufficient evidence that alignment improved.
