## Question

Why is LayerNorm placed pre-block in modern transformers, and what is RMSNorm?

## Brief answer

Pre-normalization applies normalization before each residual sublayer, which often improves optimization of deep transformers. RMSNorm rescales using root-mean-square magnitude without subtracting the mean, reducing normalization work.

## Detailed answer

A pre-norm block has the form $x+F(\operatorname{Norm}(x))$, retaining a direct residual path through depth. Post-norm applies normalization after the addition and can require different initialization or warmup. RMSNorm uses $x/\sqrt{\operatorname{mean}(x^2)+\epsilon}$ followed by learned scaling. It is not identical to LayerNorm because centering is omitted. Neither choice universally wins: stability, numerical precision and model recipe matter.

For pre-norm, the derivative through $y=x+F(\operatorname{Norm}(x))$ contains an identity contribution. This direct route helps gradients propagate across many residual blocks even when the learned branch has an unfavorable scale. In post-norm, the normalization Jacobian also lies on the residual path, changing optimization behavior. This explains a common stability advantage without proving that every pre-norm recipe has better final quality.

LayerNorm subtracts a feature-wise mean and divides by the standard deviation. RMSNorm divides by the root mean square without centering. For $x=[3,4]$, the RMS is $\sqrt{12.5}\approx3.536$, giving approximately $[0.849,1.131]$ before learned scaling. Ignoring epsilon and affine parameters, LayerNorm would instead produce $[-1,1]$. RMSNorm therefore preserves information about a nonzero common offset that LayerNorm removes.

The epsilon prevents division by a vanishing denominator, and the learned per-feature scale restores useful representational flexibility. Reduction precision matters because squaring large values in a low-precision format can overflow. Implementations often accumulate normalization statistics at higher precision. Normalization choice interacts with residual scaling, initialization, depth and learning-rate warmup; changing it in a pretrained model without retraining is not a drop-in numerical optimization.
