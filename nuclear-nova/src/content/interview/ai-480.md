## Question

You have two days in an unfamiliar codebase and no assigned task. What do you build, and how do you spend the time?

## Brief answer

Spend the first part understanding the product and finding a small, observable source of user friction; then ship one scoped improvement with tests and a clear handoff. Choose a task that fits the available evidence and time rather than a speculative rewrite. Explain the decision process without inventing prior experience in the codebase.

## Detailed answer

**Response framework.** On day one, run the product, read contribution guidance, trace one user journey, and talk with an owner about recent bugs or support requests. Identify two or three candidate improvements and choose one with a reproducible problem, bounded dependencies, and a measurable outcome. Reserve time to learn the test and deployment path.

On day two, implement the smallest useful change, add a regression test when appropriate, and demonstrate the before/after behavior. Document remaining risks and decisions for the maintainer. A good example might be fixing a confusing error path or an expensive repeated request; avoid claiming a specific defect without observing it. Success is a useful, reviewable contribution and better understanding of the system, not maximum lines of code.

### Make the choice visible

Use a short selection rubric: demonstrated user impact, reproducibility, implementation uncertainty, and ease of verification. A cosmetic issue with no observed impact may be easy but unhelpful; a broad architectural problem may be valuable but too uncertain for two days. A repeated failed import with an opaque error message can be a better choice because the problem and the improvement are both observable.

An illustrative plan is to spend the first morning reproducing that failure and tracing its request path, the afternoon agreeing on the error contract and a narrow fix, and the second day implementing, checking neighboring cases, and handing off the result. If the environment is still not runnable by midday, change scope to removing that onboarding blocker rather than concealing the delay.

In an interview, distinguish this hypothetical plan from a project you actually completed. Explain what evidence would make you stop or choose a different task. The convincing part is the reasoning that turns uncertain initial access into a useful, bounded contribution, together with a demonstration another engineer can reproduce.
