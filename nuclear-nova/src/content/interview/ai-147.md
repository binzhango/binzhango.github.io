## Question

Your agent reads inbound email and can send replies and search internal docs. Walk me through the prompt-injection attack surface and your defences.

## Brief answer

Email bodies, attachments, quoted threads, search snippets and internal documents are all untrusted inputs. An attacker can place instructions there that try to redirect the agent, exfiltrate internal data or induce an unauthorized reply. Keep content separate from policy, enforce tool permissions in code and verify the recipient and content of consequential sends against user authorization.

## Detailed answer

Trace the attack paths: an inbound email asks the assistant to search payroll records; a retrieved document claims to be a system update; an attachment embeds a URL that leaks data when fetched. The model may summarize those instructions but must not acquire authority from them.

Use least-privilege retrieval, sender/context labeling, network egress restrictions and typed tool schemas. Sending should require a user-approved purpose and appropriate destination constraints. For sensitive workflows, present the exact draft and recipient for review. Never let a model-supplied “approved” field bypass the authorization layer.

Test indirect injections inside apparently relevant evidence, not only direct jailbreak prompts. Log the data accessed and actions attempted, redact secrets and support cancellation. Classifiers and prompt reminders can reduce failures but are not complete defenses; the security boundary is what the tools permit when the model follows malicious text.

The dangerous combination is a source the attacker controls and tools the attacker cannot directly access. An email can supply a plausible business pretext, then ask the agent to retrieve internal material and quote it in a reply. The sender's request must not inherit the authenticated user's full authority merely because the user asked the assistant to summarize or triage inbox contents.

Bind sending to an authorized workflow and verify exact recipients, attachments and quoted internal data. Restrict internal searches by the user's scope and task need, and block arbitrary outbound fetches that could encode secrets in URLs. Preserve the distinction between trusted user instructions, external mail, quoted prior messages and tool results through context assembly.

Evaluate multi-step attempts where the malicious instruction is only one part of an otherwise legitimate message. Test forwarded threads, attachments and search snippets, plus uncertain send outcomes after timeouts. A durable action ledger and idempotency prevent duplicate replies during recovery. The final defense must remain effective even if the model proposes the attacker's desired action.
