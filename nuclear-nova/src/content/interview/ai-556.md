## Question

A whole-body controller trained entirely in simulation has to run on real hardware. What transfers, what does not, and how do you close the gap?

## Brief answer

Transfer task logic and useful representations, but expect gaps in contact, friction, actuator dynamics, latency, sensing, and calibration. Train with randomized plausible dynamics and validate the deployment timing explicitly. Real hardware needs an independent safety layer and staged tests; simulation success alone is insufficient evidence for operation near people.

## Detailed answer

**Suggested approach.** Identify uncertain physical parameters from hardware measurements, randomize them during training, and include sensor noise, delay, dropped observations, saturation, and disturbances. Match action units and control frequency exactly. Start with constrained motions and progressively expand the operating envelope while logging forces, tracking error, and interventions.

Use system identification and targeted real data to close observed gaps rather than broad randomization without evidence. Separate policy failures from calibration, firmware, and mechanical wear. Evaluate stability, recovery, energy, and task success across payloads and surfaces. If a policy exploits simulator artifacts, revise the simulator and retrain. Keep a tested fallback controller and safe stop behavior outside the learned policy's discretionary output.

### Identify the mismatch before widening randomization

Start with a deployment parity checklist: observation units, coordinate frames, normalization, sensor timestamps, action scaling, control frequency, and actuator limits. A meters-versus-centimeters mistake or an extra frame of delay can dominate the apparent sim-to-real gap and will not be fixed by adding visual textures to training.

Measure simple controlled responses on hardware to estimate uncertain dynamics, such as actuator lag, friction ranges, and tracking error. Randomize around physically plausible ranges and include combinations expected in deployment. Excessively broad randomization can make learning unnecessarily difficult or encourage overly conservative behavior, while a narrow range can leave the policy brittle.

Inspect whether simulation rewards an exploit. A grasp may succeed only because simulated contact is too sticky, or a gait may depend on unrealistically instantaneous torque. Reproduce the relevant motion under constrained hardware conditions, compare traces, and correct the model or action interface before scaling testing.

Progress from component checks to limited tasks and then broader conditions, retaining independent stop and fallback mechanisms. Record the exact simulator, policy, firmware, calibration, and hardware state for each result. Transfer is established for an operating envelope, not for “the real world” in general. New payloads, surfaces, wear, or temperature can require fresh validation even when the task description stays the same.
