## Question

You have 10 hours of demonstrations for a new task and budget for 50 more. How do you decide what to collect, and what return do you expect?

## Brief answer

Use the initial ten hours to train a baseline and identify specific failure clusters, then allocate the next fifty hours to coverage and recovery with measured marginal value. Reserve a held-out evaluation set before collection. Do not promise a fixed accuracy gain from hours alone; demonstration quality, diversity, and task difficulty determine the return.

## Detailed answer

**Suggested approach.** Evaluate across objects, poses, lighting, operators, and initial states, and classify failures into perception, grasp/contact, sequencing, and recovery. Collect a small tranche targeted at the largest reducible failure class, retrain, and measure improvement with uncertainty. Repeat this allocation cycle rather than spending all fifty hours up front.

Keep a fraction for broad coverage and rare but important recovery states so optimization does not overfit the current benchmark. Track accepted data, intervention burden, and learning curves by condition. If progress saturates, investigate model capacity, action representation, or calibration before collecting more of the same behavior. A useful forecast is a range based on observed tranche-to-tranche gains, explicitly qualified by the evaluation distribution.

### Spend the budget as a sequence of experiments

After the initial ten hours, freeze an evaluation panel before using its failure categories to guide collection. Suppose failures cluster around reflective objects, off-center grasps, and recovery after a slip. Allocate a small next tranche across these hypotheses and a general-coverage control rather than collecting fifty hours of the easiest successful motion.

Retrain under comparable conditions and measure changes by slice, with uncertainty from the limited trial count. If targeted off-center examples improve that slice but reduce nominal success, investigate sampling balance or conflicting demonstrations. If no slice improves, check action timing, model capacity, and label quality before purchasing more identical data.

A practical budget can reserve several hours for final held-out evaluation and distribute the rest through repeated collection decisions. The exact allocation depends on physical trial cost and risk; it is a proposal to test, not a universal ratio. Keep rare but important recovery states in the plan even when their immediate average-success gain is small.

Forecast return from the observed learning curve and identify what would invalidate it. Doubling demonstrations does not imply halving error: coverage may saturate, new objects may introduce a different failure mechanism, or operator quality may change. Report expected improvement as a conditional range and tie the next tranche to a specific uncertainty it will resolve.
