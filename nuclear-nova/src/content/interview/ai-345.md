## Question

How would you design a recommendation system to suggest books to users? How would you model a warehouse inventory problem?

## Brief answer

Suggested approach: These are two distinct systems. A book recommender ranks candidate books using user intent, history, item content, and availability. A warehouse inventory model forecasts demand and recommends replenishment subject to lead times, service levels, storage, and cost. Clarify the objective before selecting algorithms; engagement and stock availability are not interchangeable targets.

## Detailed answer

For books, retrieve candidates from collaborative signals, semantic similarity, popularity, and editorial sources. Rank for relevant long-term satisfaction, enforce availability and diversity, and handle new users with stated preferences. Use time-based offline evaluation and online experiments that avoid popularity feedback loops.

For inventory, forecast demand distributions rather than only point estimates, estimate supplier lead-time uncertainty, and choose reorder policies under stockout and holding costs. Account for substitutions, seasonality, promotions, returns, and censored demand when an item was unavailable. Backtest decisions over historical periods with realistic constraints, then pilot against service level, stockouts, waste, and working capital. A forecasting improvement matters only if it improves the downstream operational decision.

### The output is a decision, not just a score

For books, a candidate generator might retrieve a few hundred titles from collaborative and content-based sources. A ranker estimates usefulness, then a reranker removes unavailable editions and limits repeated authors or genres. A new user can supply a few preferences; a new title can use metadata before interactions accumulate. Evaluate whether recommendations lead to satisfying reading, not only attractive covers being clicked.

For inventory, suppose lead-time demand averages 100 units but varies materially. A reorder point can combine expected lead-time demand with safety stock chosen for a target service level. The exact level depends on the demand distribution, lead-time uncertainty, and shortage versus holding costs. A point forecast of 100 alone cannot express that risk. Backtest the ordering policy, including stockouts that censor observed demand and constraints such as minimum order quantities. The recommendation problem ranks optional choices; inventory optimization commits scarce capital under uncertain future demand.
