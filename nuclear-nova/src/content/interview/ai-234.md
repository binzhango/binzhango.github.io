## Question

Tell me about a time you drove a significant result through ambiguity, and a time you were wrong.

## Brief answer

Prepare two real examples: one showing how you turned an ambiguous objective into a measurable result, and one showing how evidence changed a mistaken decision. Explain the decisions and communication, including what you would do differently.

## Detailed answer

For ambiguity, describe the missing information, stakeholders, reversible experiment and success criterion you established. Show how you kept work moving while avoiding a premature large commitment. For being wrong, name your initial belief, the disconfirming evidence, how quickly you corrected it and the effect on others. Avoid disguising a strength as a mistake. Quantify outcomes only where you have records, and acknowledge shared contributions. The lesson should explain a changed practice, such as earlier validation or clearer assumptions, rather than simply saying you learned to communicate.

### Use a turning point in each example

For ambiguity, identify what was genuinely unknown: the target user, success measure, ownership, or feasible approach. Explain the small reversible step that produced information and how that evidence changed the plan. **Illustrative pattern:** prototype one workflow with operators, discover that manual review dominates effort, and shift from automated answers to better evidence collection.

For being wrong, choose a real belief with consequences and name the disconfirming observation. An example structure is choosing a shared queue for simplicity, then finding it causes cross-customer head-of-line blocking under realistic load. Describe acknowledgment, containment, repair, and the later safeguard. These illustrations are response patterns, not personal history. Avoid claiming that every risky decision was a mistake merely because an experiment failed; distinguish a reasonable uncertain bet from ignoring available evidence. Finish each story with actual outcomes and a changed practice that can be observed, such as earlier representative load testing or explicit decision criteria.
