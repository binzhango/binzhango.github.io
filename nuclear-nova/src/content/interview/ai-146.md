## Question

How would you design an experiment to test for a specific emergent capability or bias in a large language model?

## Brief answer

Operationalize the suspected capability or bias into measurable behavior, preregister comparisons and test on held-out cases. Vary the suspected causal factor while controlling confounders, use enough repeated samples to estimate uncertainty, and include negative controls. A few striking examples can generate a hypothesis but cannot establish its prevalence or cause.

## Detailed answer

For a bias study, create matched prompts differing only in the attribute under investigation, then have blinded reviewers score a defined outcome. Also test naturally occurring data because synthetic matched prompts may not represent deployment. Report subgroup sample sizes, effect sizes and uncertainty rather than only an overall p-value.

For an emergent capability, compare checkpoints or scales under equal scaffolding, tool access and inference budgets. Use continuous scores alongside pass/fail thresholds to distinguish true qualitative changes from measurement artifacts. Hold out task families to reduce contamination and benchmark-specific training effects.

Record prompts, sampling settings, model versions and scoring rubrics. Correct for multiple comparisons when exploring many conditions. Replicate concerning results independently and test proposed mitigations on both target failures and ordinary usefulness. Absence of observed failures bounds risk only for the tested distribution and statistical power.

For a matched-prompt bias test, keep qualification, task difficulty and response format constant while varying the attribute of interest. Randomize presentation order and blind reviewers to the experimental condition where possible. A measured difference is easier to interpret when alternative explanations such as name frequency, dialect or prompt length have been controlled rather than changed accidentally.

For capability emergence, use several difficulty levels and continuous measures such as partial progress or probability assigned to a correct answer. Compare fixed inference budgets as well as deployment-realistic budgets; otherwise a larger model receiving more attempts can be mistaken for a pure scaling effect.

Predefine the primary comparison and minimum meaningful effect. If exploratory testing scans many attributes and prompts, report that exploration and confirm findings on a fresh set. Repeated samples from one prompt do not replace coverage of different tasks. The resulting claim should match the experiment's scope: evidence of a disparity on a particular test distribution is not a complete explanation of its cause or prevalence in every deployment.
