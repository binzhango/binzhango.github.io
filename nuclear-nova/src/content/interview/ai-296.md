## Question

Qwen uses byte-level BPE with a ~151K vocabulary, augmented for multilingual coverage and with digits split into single characters. Why those choices, and what are the trade-offs?

## Brief answer

Byte-level BPE provides byte coverage, while a large multilingual vocabulary can encode common non-English text with fewer tokens. Splitting digits individually gives a regular representation of numbers instead of arbitrary multi-digit chunks. These choices trade sequence length and coverage against embedding size, output-softmax cost, and less compact numerical strings.

## Detailed answer

The exact vocabulary and pre-tokenizer depend on the Qwen checkpoint; inspect its tokenizer files rather than applying one family-wide number. Qwen2.5 describes a roughly 151K-token vocabulary.

Evaluate token counts on native text, code, punctuation, rare characters, and code-switching. A vocabulary dominated by one language makes others pay more tokens for the same semantic content, increasing context use and serving cost. Single-digit splitting avoids inconsistent boundaries such as one frequent three-digit number becoming one token and another becoming several, but does not make arithmetic reliable by itself. Larger vocabularies increase parameter and logit memory. Compare end-task quality and latency rather than minimizing fertility alone; normalization or pre-tokenization errors can erase any vocabulary advantage.

### Vocabulary size changes two costs

The embedding table has roughly vocabulary size times hidden width parameters, and the output projection must score the vocabulary unless an alternative is used. At width 4,096, a 151,000-token table has about 618.5 million entries; at two bytes per entry that is roughly 1.24 GB. Tied input/output weights avoid storing a second independent matrix, but the output computation remains.

Larger multilingual coverage can reduce sequence length enough to offset some of that expense. Digit-level splitting gives “123” the same three boundaries as an unfamiliar three-digit number, making place-value patterns more consistent; it also spends more tokens on numeric-heavy text. Byte fallback ensures encodability of unfamiliar characters, but may require many tokens for a single grapheme. Compare matched documents and complete tasks with identical semantic content. Fertility, output-projection cost, rare-script quality, and arithmetic performance must be measured together rather than using vocabulary size as a quality score.
