## Question

How do you evaluate a manipulation policy when every trial costs robot time and every failure has physical consequences?

## Brief answer

Use a staged evaluation ladder: offline checks and simulation, constrained hardware trials, then broader conditions only after safety gates pass. Define task success, failure severity, intervention, and recovery before testing. Allocate scarce robot time to representative and uncertainty-reducing cases while retaining an unbiased panel for estimating real performance.

## Detailed answer

**Suggested approach.** Stratify trials by object, pose, environment, payload, and known failure mode. Randomize trial order and reset conditions consistently; log policy version and hardware state. Use trained operators, independent emergency controls, and predefined stop rules. Never continue a dangerous run simply to obtain a complete metric.

Report success with confidence intervals, near-hazard events, interventions, damage, and cycle time. Separate targeted stress tests from representative-rate estimates so oversampled difficult cases do not distort deployment frequency. Use simulation to prioritize hypotheses, then confirm critical mechanisms on hardware. Release only within the tested operating envelope and preserve failed trajectories for review and targeted data collection.

### Separate estimating performance from finding failures

A representative test panel estimates behavior under the intended deployment distribution. A stress panel deliberately targets difficult or uncertain conditions to uncover weaknesses. Both are necessary, but their success rates answer different questions. Mixing them without accounting for sampling can make a policy appear to improve or degrade simply because the test mix changed.

For policy comparisons, use matched task conditions and randomize execution order where practical. Record resets, object placement, battery or thermal state, and hardware condition so a later run is not unfairly disadvantaged. Predetermine stop rules and count interventions consistently; excluding rescued trials can hide the failures most relevant to deployment.

Small samples constrain conclusions. With zero failures in 30 independent representative trials, the approximate rule-of-three upper 95% bound on failure probability is 3/30, or 10%. That does not establish rare-event safety. Dependence between repeated trials can make the effective evidence weaker still.

Use simulation and offline checks to eliminate obvious regressions before consuming hardware time, then allocate physical trials to important uncertainties and representative coverage. Preserve failed trajectories for analysis rather than merely rerunning until success. Release evidence should specify the tested conditions, statistical limitations, observed interventions, and unresolved hazards. A single aggregate task-success number cannot replace that structured assessment.
