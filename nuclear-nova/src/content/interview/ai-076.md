## Question

Design online evaluation: what do you log, what do you sample, and what do you A/B?

## Brief answer

Online evaluation combines sampled quality review with randomized experiments on meaningful user outcomes. Log enough context to compare versions while minimizing sensitive-data retention and accounting for exposure and selection bias.

## Detailed answer

Choose the randomization unit before rollout, often user or account, and avoid users switching variants mid-workflow. Track completion, corrections, escalation, latency and cost, plus predefined safety guardrails. Sample low-confidence and high-impact cases separately from a representative baseline. Run long enough to cover normal traffic cycles and inspect segment effects. Thumbs-up rate alone is biased because only some users provide feedback.

Choose the experimental unit to match interference. Randomizing individual requests can expose one user to inconsistent behavior during a multi-step task; user- or account-level assignment may be more appropriate. Persist assignment and log actual exposure so failed routing or fallback does not silently mix treatments. Decide success metrics and safety guardrails before launching.

For a support assistant, measure resolved cases, repeat contacts, human escalation, corrections, latency and cost. A lower escalation rate is not automatically good if the model simply stops recognizing cases that need help. Sample conversations for independent quality review, including a random baseline and targeted high-risk cases. Report the two samples separately or weight them correctly when estimating production error rates.

Run long enough to cover relevant traffic cycles and assess uncertainty rather than stopping as soon as a favorable number appears. Inspect important slices and delayed outcomes. Shadow evaluation is useful for read-only output comparison but cannot fully measure user response to a new behavior; action-taking experiments need stricter controls. Qualitative feedback explains failures that aggregate metrics miss, while randomized outcomes help avoid selection bias in voluntary thumbs-up data. Use both to decide whether the change improves the real task.
