## Question

Explain the LoRA decomposition mathematically. Why does it work, and how do you choose the rank r?

## Brief answer

LoRA learns a low-rank update $\Delta W=(\alpha/r)BA$ while keeping the base matrix frozen. For a $d_{out}\times d_{in}$ weight, the adapters train $r(d_{in}+d_{out})$ parameters instead of the full product.

## Detailed answer

Here $A$ has shape $r\times d_{in}$ and $B$ has shape $d_{out}\times r$. Common initialization makes the initial update zero, preserving the pretrained function. Rank limits update capacity; increase it when held-out quality justifies the memory and compute, rather than assuming larger is always better. Target-module selection and data quality can matter more than rank.

For a $4096\times4096$ matrix, full tuning changes 16,777,216 parameters. At rank 16, LoRA trains $16(4096+4096)=131,072$, or about 0.78% as many. During a forward pass, compute the original projection and add the adapter path: $Wx+(\alpha/r)B(Ax)$. The update has rank at most $r$, although the combined matrix $W+\Delta W$ can still be full rank.

The useful hypothesis is that adapting a pretrained representation often requires changes in fewer directions than learning the representation from scratch. It is an empirical property of tasks and pretrained models, not a theorem that all useful updates are low rank. If the task demands new representations or substantial domain shift, a small rank may underfit.

Choose rank with a held-out sweep while holding data, update budget and target modules controlled. Compare adapting attention projections, MLP projections or both; module choice can matter more than a small rank change. Monitor general capability retention and overfitting as well as target accuracy. For deployment, compatible adapters can be merged into base weights to remove the extra matrix path, but merging into quantized weights can introduce another rounding step and should be evaluated separately.
