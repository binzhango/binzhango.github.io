## Question

Design a customer-facing agent for an airline that can cancel and rebook flights. How do you keep it from violating fare policy?

## Brief answer

Let the model interpret the request and explain options, while a deterministic booking service enforces fare rules, passenger authorization, prices, and transaction state. Rebooking requires an explicit proposed itinerary and confirmed user approval before committing consequential changes. Cancellation and purchase should not become an uncontrolled sequence of independent tool calls.

## Detailed answer

**Suggested approach.** Fetch the authoritative reservation and applicable fare conditions, compute permitted alternatives in code, and present price differences, penalties, and refund treatment. Use a short-lived quote and revalidate availability and price at commit. Prefer a backend exchange transaction; if only separate operations exist, model the saga and compensation limits explicitly.

Idempotency keys and a transaction ledger prevent duplicate charges and track unknown outcomes after timeouts. Escalate complex cases, policy ambiguity, or failed compensation to a human with full state. Test a fare changing during approval, one segment unavailable, passenger mismatch, repeated requests, and interrupted payment. Measure correct resolution, unauthorized changes, stranded itineraries, and handoff quality. The agent's prose cannot override the fare engine or promise a refund before the system confirms it.

### Design the transaction before the conversation

Model the proposed exchange as a structured object containing passenger, original booking, target segments, fare-rule decision, total price difference, and quote expiry. User approval binds to that object. If inventory or price changes before execution, invalidate the quote and obtain fresh approval rather than silently substituting a more expensive itinerary.

When the backend supports an atomic exchange, delegate the operation to it and reconcile by a durable transaction ID. If it exposes only separate cancellation and purchase calls, explicitly identify the failure states. Canceling first may leave the traveler stranded; purchasing first may create duplicate commitments. A saga can record compensation steps, but some fare changes are not reversible, so “rollback” is not a magic guarantee.

The agent may explain available choices and collect missing information, while the policy and booking services decide eligibility and execute writes. Test a successful exchange, expired quote, partial itinerary failure, lost acknowledgment, and repeated approval message. Customer-facing success should depend on the confirmed new itinerary and known disposition of the old one, not on completion of the model's planned tool sequence.
