## Question

Define the bias-variance trade-off and discuss the relationship between the two.

## Brief answer

Bias is systematic error from the learning procedure's assumptions; variance is sensitivity to the sampled training data. In the squared-error decomposition, expected prediction error contains squared bias, variance and irreducible noise. Increasing flexibility often reduces bias and increases variance, but this is a tendency under particular conditions, not a universal law for every modern model.

## Detailed answer

For a fixed input x and noisy target $Y=f(x)+\epsilon$ with zero-mean noise independent of training, the expected squared prediction error is

$$\mathbb{E}[(Y-\hat f(x))^2]=\operatorname{Bias}(\hat f(x))^2+\operatorname{Var}(\hat f(x))+\sigma^2.$$

The expectation is over hypothetical training datasets and test noise. A rigid linear model on a nonlinear target may have high bias; a highly flexible model trained on few noisy examples may vary dramatically between samples. Regularization, more data and ensembling affect these terms differently.

Use learning curves and held-out errors to diagnose the practical problem. High training and validation error suggests underfitting or poor features; a large generalization gap suggests overfitting, though distribution shift can produce a similar pattern. Classification losses have different decompositions, and overparameterized models can show double-descent behavior. Do not infer bias and variance solely from parameter count.

Bias and variance concern a learning procedure across possible training samples, not just one fitted model's residuals. Imagine repeatedly collecting datasets and fitting the same polynomial degree. A low-degree fit may miss a curved trend consistently, while a high-degree fit may follow noise differently in each sample. Their average predictions and spread define the two terms.

More data often reduces variance without requiring a simpler model. Bagging reduces variance when member errors are not perfectly correlated; regularization usually trades some added bias for lower sensitivity. Better features can reduce bias without merely adding parameters. These interventions explain why “increase complexity until the curves meet” is too simplistic.

Use training-size and regularization learning curves with deployment-like validation splits. A generalization gap may arise from distribution shift or leakage mistakes as well as variance. For modern overparameterized models, interpolation and implicit optimization biases can produce behavior outside the classic U-shaped cartoon. The squared-error decomposition remains useful under its assumptions, but model selection still requires measured performance and error analysis.
