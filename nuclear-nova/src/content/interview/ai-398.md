## Question

Tell me about a model you shipped that made a measurable business difference, and one that did not.

## Brief answer

Response framework: contrast two real model deployments using the same causal discipline: intended decision, baseline, adoption, measured outcome, and uncertainty. For the successful model, explain why you believe it caused the improvement. For the unsuccessful one, distinguish poor model quality from integration, objective, data, or organizational failures and describe what you learned.

## Detailed answer

State your role and the model's place in the workflow. Use actual experiment results or clearly qualified observational evidence, including time period and guardrails. An offline metric improvement is not automatically a business effect; account for exposure, user behavior, and concurrent changes.

For the model that did not help, explain the evidence that changed your view, whether you stopped or redesigned it, and what cost was avoided or incurred. Avoid blaming users for not adopting a tool that did not fit their work. End with a concrete principle you now apply, such as validating the decision pathway before investing in model complexity. Do not invent impressive numbers to make the story symmetrical.

### Make the two stories comparable

For the successful model, explain the decision it changed and how that change affected the business. **Illustrative structure:** a prioritization model moved scarce review effort toward cases with higher recoverable value, and a controlled test measured the improvement while tracking false positives. Replace the scenario and metrics with your actual work.

For the unsuccessful model, identify the broken link: perhaps an offline accuracy gain did not change staff decisions, or added latency outweighed relevance. Explain how you discovered that, whether you stopped the rollout, and what you changed in the product or evaluation. Do not retroactively call every inconclusive experiment a success. Use causal evidence when available; if results are observational, state competing explanations such as seasonality or simultaneous releases. The strongest comparison shows that you now validate the decision pathway and adoption conditions before investing heavily in modeling, while still learning from well-designed experiments that fail.
