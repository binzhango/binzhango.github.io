## Question

Walk me through matrix manipulations relevant to LLM architectures.

## Brief answer

Track every axis before manipulating a tensor: batch, sequence, head and feature dimensions. Linear projections multiply the final feature axis; reshapes split dimensions; transposes change axis order; attention matrix multiplication contracts the head dimension. Many silent LLM bugs come from a valid broadcast over the wrong axis rather than an invalid shape.

## Detailed answer

For input $X\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times T\times D}$ and $H$ heads with $d=D/H$, projection gives Q, K and V shaped `(B,T,D)`. Reshape to `(B,T,H,d)` and transpose to `(B,H,T,d)`. Then $QK^\top$ has shape `(B,H,T,T)`, softmax runs over the final key-position axis, and multiplying by V returns `(B,H,T,d)`. Transpose and combine heads before the output projection.

In GQA, K/V have $G<H$ heads, so query head $h$ maps to a KV group. In tensor parallelism, column-sharding a projection partitions output features; a following row-sharded projection usually requires a reduction across ranks. These are mathematical partitions, not arbitrary chunks of flattened memory.

Check a small example with distinct batch/head values to catch accidental broadcasting. Verify contiguity requirements before `.view`, distinguish transpose from inverse, and keep masking aligned with absolute token positions during cached decoding. Document shapes next to each matrix multiplication; it is more useful than memorizing a long sequence of reshapes.

A reshape preserves element order while changing how dimensions group those elements; a transpose changes which axis is traversed first. Confusing them can exchange head and token identities without producing a shape error. After transposing, memory may be noncontiguous, explaining why some frameworks require a contiguous copy before a view-based flattening operation.

For $B=2$, $T=3$, $H=4$ and $d=5$, attention scores have shape `[2,4,3,3]`. The final two axes mean query position and key position. Softmax over the query axis would answer a different question and generally fail the intended weighted-read semantics. Cached decode changes these to `[2,4,1,S]`, where $S$ includes history.

Use small tensors whose values encode their indices, then inspect a few entries after each reshape and transpose. This catches accidental broadcasting across batches and wrong head-group mappings more reliably than random shape assertions alone. For distributed projections, verify partial results against an unsharded matrix multiplication before optimizing communication.
