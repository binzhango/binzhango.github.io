## Question

A freight rail operator loses tens of millions a year to unplanned locomotive downtime. Decompose this into an engineering plan.

## Brief answer

Translate downtime into specific failure modes, operational decisions, and measurable loss before building a predictive model. Join maintenance, fault codes, sensor telemetry, usage, parts, and dispatch data by reliable asset identity and time. The objective is fewer costly disruptions through actionable interventions, not merely a high anomaly-detection score.

## Detailed answer

**Suggested approach.** Baseline downtime hours, failure frequency, repair lead time, false alarms, and cost by locomotive class and component. Identify one failure mode with useful warning lead time and a feasible maintenance action. Build time-aware training labels that avoid leaking post-failure repairs into prediction, and compare against simple rules.

Pilot alerts in shadow mode, then integrate reviewed work orders with parts and crew availability. Evaluate precision at the maintenance team's capacity, avoided downtime, unnecessary maintenance, and operational disruption. Use prospective or controlled rollout evidence where possible because historical correlations can overstate savings. Monitor sensor drift and fleet changes. A model that predicts failure after the last practical intervention point has little business value.

### Choose an intervention with enough lead time

Suppose compressor failures account for a substantial share of lost service and maintenance needs twelve hours to obtain parts and schedule a crew. A predictor that becomes accurate ten minutes before failure cannot deliver the intended saving. Define the target as actionable warning within a useful horizon, and build labels that distinguish failure, preventive replacement, and censored units still operating.

Join telemetry, fault codes, maintenance history, asset configuration, and operating conditions using reliable time and identity. Compare a simple rule baseline with a model, then estimate precision at the maintenance team's daily capacity. The product path is alert → review → parts/crew check → work order → completed intervention, with outcomes logged at each stage. Pilot prospectively to measure avoided downtime and unnecessary maintenance, accounting for intervention changing the observed failure label. Track warning lead time and operational adoption alongside model scores. Start with one failure mode whose data and intervention are credible before expanding to a fleet-wide predictive-maintenance platform.
