## Question

What problems exist in PDF parsing?

## Brief answer

Common PDF parsing problems include broken reading order, missing text, OCR errors, split tables, repeated headers, and lost structural relationships.

## Detailed answer

Two-column pages can interleave sentences; ligatures and embedded fonts can corrupt characters; scans can erase decimal points. Captions may detach from figures, and tables can lose row/column associations. Use page-level quality checks, compare suspicious regions with images, and exclude repeated boilerplate where appropriate. Parsing errors propagate into embeddings and answers, so fixing them upstream often matters more than switching generators.

A common failure chain begins with layout. A parser alternates lines from two columns, producing grammatical fragments that still look like text. The embedding model then represents those fragments, and retrieval returns passages that never existed as coherent statements. Another failure is repeated headers and footers dominating many chunks, increasing similarity without adding useful content.

Numbers deserve targeted checks. OCR can confuse `O` with zero, lose a decimal point, or attach a footnote marker to a value. A multi-page table can repeat headers or continue a row on the next page, and treating each page independently can misassign values. Hyphenation at line endings may be either a true hyphen or a word break.

Use page-type detection, reading-order checks, repeated-region removal, and table validation where applicable. Compare suspicious output against the rendered page and retain extraction confidence and provenance. Validate representative documents before indexing an entire archive. When an answer is wrong, inspect the parsed source first: a generator cannot reliably restore a missing decimal or qualification from context, regardless of how fluent its explanation is.
