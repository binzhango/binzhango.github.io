## Question

Design dynamic pricing / surge for a two-sided marketplace and describe the feedback loops that can go wrong.

## Brief answer

Suggested approach: estimate supply-demand imbalance and elasticity, then choose bounded price adjustments to improve marketplace balance under explicit user and policy constraints. Prices change behavior, so the system is a feedback controller as well as a predictor. Optimizing immediate revenue alone can produce oscillation, strategic gaming, unfair exposure, or long-term loss of trust.

## Detailed answer

Model demand, available supply, travel times, and response to price using experiments or credible causal estimates. Separate forecasting from the optimization policy. Smooth noisy signals, cap update rates and magnitudes, and account for delayed driver and rider responses. Include geographic spillovers and incentives that shift supply from neighboring areas.

Evaluate marketplace-wide outcomes such as fulfillment, wait time, cancellations, participant earnings, and user retention. User-level A/B tests may suffer interference; geographic clusters or switchbacks can better match the mechanism. Monitor runaway loops where higher prices reduce observed requests and corrupt demand estimates. Keep manual controls and rollback for outages or unusual events, and review applicable pricing constraints before deployment.

### Pricing is a control loop

A price increase can reduce demand and attract supply, but those responses occur with delays. If the controller reacts too aggressively to each short-term imbalance, it can oscillate: prices rise, drivers move in, demand drops, prices collapse, and drivers leave. Smooth estimates, bound changes, and model response delays rather than optimizing each instant independently.

Separate latent demand from observed requests, since users deterred by price may never create a request record. Estimate elasticity through controlled variation where appropriate, accounting for geographic spillovers and concurrent incentives. Optimize a constrained objective such as fulfillment and participant welfare subject to service, affordability, and operational limits. Test policies through simulation calibrated to real behavior, then controlled cluster or switchback experiments. Monitor cancellations, wait times, driver earnings, and neighboring-zone effects. A policy that raises short-term revenue while degrading supply reliability may be counterproductive. Keep manual override and clearly defined abnormal-event behavior.
