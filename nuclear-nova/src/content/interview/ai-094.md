## Question

How do you handle code-switching and accents in a production ASR system?

## Brief answer

Use representative multilingual and accent coverage, explicit code-switching tests and adaptation focused on observed errors. Avoid forcing every utterance into one language when speakers naturally mix languages.

## Detailed answer

Measure entity, number and intent accuracy by language pair, speaker and acoustic condition. Supply bounded contextual vocabulary for domain terms, but test whether biasing causes false insertions. Improve data coverage and decoding before assuming a larger model fixes the problem. Support clarification for low-confidence critical content and preserve the original audio for authorized review. Evaluate real code-switch boundaries rather than only separate monolingual recordings.

Treat code-switching as within-utterance behavior. A speaker may use one language for grammar and another for a product name, address or technical term. Forcing a single language ID at the beginning can bias later decoding toward the wrong vocabulary. Evaluate mixed utterances with realistic switch locations and acoustic conditions, not a concatenation of clean monolingual clips alone.

Use training and adaptation data that cover supported accents and language pairs. Add contextual vocabulary for likely names or domain terms where the recognizer supports it, then test false insertions: strong biasing can hallucinate a favored term in unrelated speech. Preserve language-specific normalization so numbers and names are not incorrectly translated or reformatted.

Measure word errors alongside entity, number and intent accuracy, since these often determine task success. Inspect whether failures come from acoustic coverage, language modeling, endpointing or normalization before increasing model size. For uncertain consequential details, ask for confirmation or a spelling instead of silently guessing. Track quality by slice after updates and provide a feedback path for recurring names and accents. Accent adaptation should improve recognition without requiring assumptions about a speaker's identity or demographic group.
