## Question

You ship a policy to 300 robots. It works in the lab and degrades in the field. Debug it.

## Brief answer

Compare field and lab distributions, hardware health, and deployment versions before blaming the policy. Correlate degradation with robot, site, task, object, lighting, network, and time since maintenance. Contain unsafe behavior through rollback or a reduced operating envelope while preserving diagnostic telemetry.

## Detailed answer

**Suggested approach.** Verify model checksum, preprocessing, calibration, control frequency, firmware, and sensor synchronization across all 300 robots. Look for camera drift, worn grippers, changed payloads, latency spikes, and task instructions differing from training. Reproduce field failures in a controlled setting with the same artifacts and compare known-good policies on matched hardware.

Separate common-mode software regressions from a subset of unhealthy devices. Collect targeted recovery data only after fixing instrumentation and hardware faults. Roll out changes through a small monitored cohort with objective stop criteria. Track success and intervention by condition, not just fleet average, and verify that telemetry itself is not biased toward connected or successful robots. A lab fix is complete only after its effect is confirmed in the affected field slice.

### Build a comparison matrix before retraining

Join every episode with robot ID, site, policy checksum, preprocessing version, calibration, hardware health, and environmental descriptors. Plot failure rates by these factors and by rollout time. If degradation begins immediately on all updated robots, suspect a common software change. If it accumulates on a subset over weeks, inspect wear, calibration drift, and maintenance patterns.

Use controlled swaps to distinguish hypotheses: run the known-good policy on affected hardware and the new policy on a healthy matched robot, inside a safe test setup. Change one factor at a time where practical. A camera mount shifted by a few degrees may look like a perception-model regression until calibration is checked.

Check telemetry completeness. Robots with poor connectivity may upload fewer failures, and successful episodes may be easier to log than aborted ones. Compare expected episode counts with received records and capture failure summaries locally for later upload.

Contain the affected slice while investigating, then canary a targeted fix with explicit rollback criteria. Retraining on field data is appropriate only after verifying that the data reflects real task variation rather than a broken sensor or inconsistent action scaling. Confirm recovery in the sites and conditions that actually regressed; a better laboratory average alone does not close the incident.
