## Question

How do LayerNorm, BatchNorm, and RMSNorm compare?

## Brief answer

LayerNorm normalizes each token across hidden features; RMSNorm rescales those features without subtracting their mean; BatchNorm uses batch-dependent statistics during training.

## Detailed answer

For token vector $x\in\mathbb R^d$:

$$
\operatorname{LN}(x)=\gamma\odot\frac{x-\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\epsilon}}+\beta,\qquad
\operatorname{RMSNorm}(x)=\gamma\odot\frac{x}{\sqrt{d^{-1}\sum_i x_i^2+\epsilon}}.
$$

BatchNorm's reduction axes depend on the layer and data layout; it commonly uses running statistics at inference. Batch dependence, padding, and train/inference differences make it awkward for autoregressive language models. RMSNorm saves centering work, but speed and training stability depend on kernels and the full architecture.

For $x=(1,3)$, ignoring epsilon and using unit scales, LayerNorm subtracts mean 2 and divides by standard deviation 1, producing $(-1,1)$. RMSNorm divides by $\sqrt{(1^2+3^2)/2}=\sqrt5$, producing approximately $(0.447,1.342)$. RMSNorm controls magnitude while retaining the common offset; LayerNorm removes that offset. Learned scale and optional bias then allow the model to adjust the normalized features.

For a Transformer tensor shaped `[batch, tokens, hidden]`, ordinary LayerNorm and RMSNorm operate independently on each token's hidden axis. Their output for one token therefore does not depend directly on the other examples sharing its batch. BatchNorm generally estimates statistics across examples and possibly spatial positions for each channel, making batching and padding relevant to the estimate.

Epsilon prevents division by zero and affects nearly constant or very small vectors. Mixed-precision kernels often accumulate reductions more accurately than the input storage dtype. Normalization placement remains a separate architectural decision: replacing LayerNorm with RMSNorm does not specify whether it occurs before or after a residual addition.
