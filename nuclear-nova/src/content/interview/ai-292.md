## Question

GLM-4.5's post-training trains expert models per domain then unifies with self-distillation. Walk through why you would train specialists and then merge them.

## Brief answer

Specialists let each domain use its own verifier, data mixture, and optimization schedule without immediately interfering with other capabilities. Distillation then trains a shared student on selected specialist outputs, aiming to retain their gains in one deployable model. This is knowledge transfer through training data or distributions, not necessarily arithmetic averaging of weights.

## Detailed answer

For example, a coding specialist can use sandboxed tests while a math specialist uses exact-answer verification. Sample tasks from each, filter erroneous or excessively long responses, and train the shared checkpoint on a balanced mixture. Re-evaluate general instruction following and domains not represented by the specialists.

The hard parts are incompatible response styles, correlated teacher mistakes, coverage loss, and a student that lacks capacity to absorb every gain. Use per-domain heldouts and compare to joint training at the same compute budget. GLM-4.5 reports expert-model iteration and self-distillation in its post-training process; that evidence should not be restated as a generic claim that weight merging is always safe.

### Distillation is not adding specialist weights

Start several specialists from a compatible checkpoint, then improve each using its domain's data and rewards. The coding specialist might produce tested patches, while the math specialist produces verified answers. A shared student learns from their successful outputs, so the unification happens through a training dataset and objective. Simply averaging independently trained parameters is a different technique and can destroy useful behavior.

For a balanced distillation set, retain task identity, specialist version, verification result, and generation cost. Sample by desired deployment coverage instead of taking every easy successful output. Evaluate whether a coding improvement causes worse conversation or multilingual behavior. If the student consistently fails one specialist's hardest problems, the issue may be insufficient capacity or poor examples rather than insufficient mixing. Compare against a shared model trained jointly on the same verified tasks; specialists add value only if their separate optimization discovers useful behavior that the shared process would otherwise miss.
