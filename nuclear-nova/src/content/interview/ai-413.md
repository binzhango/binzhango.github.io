## Question

A 70B dense model at 8-bit weights, chips with ~230 MB of SRAM each. Walk me through the deployment and the unit economics.

## Brief answer

Under the stated assumptions, 70B eight-bit weights occupy about 70 GB, so 230 MB of SRAM per chip implies at least $\lceil70{,}000/230\rceil=305$ chips for raw weights alone. Real deployment needs more capacity for scales, activations, buffers, and any resident KV cache, plus a feasible communication schedule. The 230 MB figure is an interview assumption, not a current-generation product specification.

## Detailed answer

Map tensor/layer shards onto the fabric, account for replication required by throughput, and estimate per-token communication and compute. Capacity is only the first constraint: a 305-chip placement may not be a supported topology or meet latency targets. Quantization metadata and inaccessible SRAM regions further change the count.

For economics, divide total annualized hardware, power, networking, operations, and redundancy costs by successful served tokens under realistic utilization. Separate reserved low-latency capacity from high-throughput shared service. Compare against GPU serving at the same model quality, context distribution, availability, and p99 target. Do not infer cost advantage from raw SRAM bandwidth or chips-per-model alone; occupancy and delivered throughput determine the denominator.

### Derive a lower bound, then add reality

At one byte per weight, 70B dense weights require 70 billion bytes. With 230 million bytes of usable SRAM per chip under the question's assumed hardware, the pure capacity lower bound is $\lceil70{,}000/230\rceil=305$ chips. If the 230 MB is a physical total rather than usable weight capacity, activations, cache, instructions, and metadata push the required count higher. Other hardware generations have different capacities; this is a scenario calculation.

A supported deployment may round up to topology-friendly groups and replicate stages for throughput or availability. Compute and network balance can require more chips than storage alone. For cost, take the total provisioned group expense per hour and divide by SLO-compliant successful output tokens per hour, including utilization and redundancy. A system with impressive per-user speed can still be expensive at low occupancy. Compare matched workloads and availability requirements, and include model-loading and compiler costs when demand changes frequently.
