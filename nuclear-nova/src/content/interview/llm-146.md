## Question

How do random negatives compare with top-k hard negatives?

## Brief answer

Random negatives are cheap and diverse but often easy; top-k mined negatives are more confusable but contain more false negatives and selection bias.

## Detailed answer

Retrieve high-scoring candidates for each training query, remove known positives, and filter or annotate the remaining candidates before treating them as negative. Start with a mixture and refresh mining as the retriever changes. The highest-scoring candidate is not automatically a valid negative. Compare gains on independent queries and track whether the mining procedure overrepresents particular document types.

Random negatives sample broadly from the corpus. They establish coarse separation cheaply, but most may be obviously unrelated once the encoder is competent. Top-k mining instead chooses passages the current model finds plausible, concentrating training on existing ranking mistakes. That can improve sample efficiency while increasing the need for careful labels.

For a query about version 3's timeout default, a version 2 manual is a useful hard negative only if it truly cannot support the version 3 answer. A mirrored page containing the same current documentation may be another positive, even if its ID differs. Use source identity, duplicate detection, and support judgments to distinguish those cases.

A practical schedule starts with reliable positives and mixed negatives, then periodically mines fresh candidates. Avoid choosing only the very highest scores, since that region often contains unlabeled positives or ambiguous evidence. Compare evaluation on a separate query set and inspect whether gains generalize beyond the miner's favorite document types. The objective is better discrimination, not maximizing training difficulty regardless of correctness.
