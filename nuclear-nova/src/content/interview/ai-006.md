## Question

How does Byte Pair Encoding work, and what are its failure modes (numbers, code, non-Latin scripts)?

## Brief answer

BPE repeatedly merges frequent adjacent symbols into a vocabulary of reusable subwords. Encoding applies the learned merge rules, so token boundaries reflect training frequency rather than linguistic or numeric structure.

## Detailed answer

Start from characters or bytes, count adjacent pairs, merge the most frequent pair, and repeat to the vocabulary budget. Encoding uses the saved merge ranking, not a fresh frequency calculation on the input. Numbers may fragment into inconsistent digit groups; rare scripts can consume many tokens; whitespace and indentation matter in code. Byte-level fallback avoids unknown bytes but does not guarantee efficient representations. Evaluate tokens per character by language and exact round-trip reconstruction.

Consider a small corpus containing repeated `low`, `lower` and `lowest`. Starting with individual symbols, merges may build `lo`, then `low`, leaving suffixes such as `er` and `est` reusable. The learned vocabulary can express an unseen word as multiple known pieces. A tokenizer also defines normalization, whitespace handling, pre-tokenization boundaries and special tokens; those choices affect results as much as the merge algorithm.

This frequency objective is not an arithmetic objective. The strings `1234` and `1235` might split differently, so a model must learn carries and place value over inconsistent groups. In code, equivalent formatting can change token counts, and splitting identifiers may hide useful lexical structure. A low-resource script represented mainly as UTF-8 bytes can require several tokens per character, reducing effective context and increasing serving cost for the same visible text.

Measure token fertility separately by language and domain, and test combining marks, emojis, source-code indentation and unusual Unicode. Round-trip reconstruction should be specified relative to any intentional normalization; a tokenizer that normalizes text cannot promise byte-for-byte recovery of the original. Changing tokenization after training also changes vocabulary IDs and learned embeddings, so it usually requires model adaptation rather than swapping a preprocessing library.
