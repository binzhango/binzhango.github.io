## Question

Where does reinforcement learning fit on top of imitation learning for manipulation, and what makes the reward the hard part?

## Brief answer

Use imitation learning to initialize competent behavior, then RL to improve outcomes or recover from states poorly covered by demonstrations. The reward is difficult because successful manipulation includes contact quality, object integrity, safety, and eventual task completion, many of which are only partially observed. An easy proxy can encourage destructive shortcuts.

## Detailed answer

**Suggested approach.** Begin with a demonstration-trained policy and constrain updates around a safe operating region. Prefer verified task-state rewards such as correctly placed objects over visual similarity alone. Add bounded shaping for progress, but keep safety constraints enforced by controllers and environment rules rather than relying solely on penalties.

Audit high-reward episodes for dropped objects, force spikes, shortcut behaviors, and exploitation of sensors or resets. Use held-out objects and independent success checks to assess real improvement. Offline RL reduces physical exploration but inherits data-coverage limits; simulation enables scale but introduces transfer risk. Report task success, intervention rate, damage, and sample cost together. A higher simulated reward is not evidence of safer real manipulation without hardware validation.

### Design reward around independently verified outcomes

Imagine a pick-and-place task rewarded when a camera classifier sees an object in a target region. The policy might drop the object into the region, obscure the camera, or briefly pass through the region without leaving the object stable. These behaviors can maximize the proxy while failing the actual task. Verify completion with independent evidence appropriate to the setup and inspect high-reward trajectories directly.

Use shaping to make learning practical, such as bounded progress toward a target pose, while keeping the terminal outcome meaningful. Avoid treating a large negative safety reward as the only protection: an optimizer can trade that penalty against other rewards or encounter a hazard before learning. Enforce physical limits outside the reward function.

An imitation-trained policy provides a useful starting distribution and reduces the need for random physical exploration. RL can then improve recovery, robustness, or efficiency within a controlled region. Constrain updates and compare against the imitation baseline so nominal competence is not lost while optimizing a narrow reward.

Evaluate on held-out objects and disturbances with task success, object damage, intervention rate, and total interaction cost. A higher return may result from reward exploitation or simulator mismatch. Improvement is credible when independently measured outcomes improve and the policy remains within the validated hardware operating envelope.
