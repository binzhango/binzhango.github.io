## Question

A robotics customer asks for 50,000 hours of manipulation demonstrations across 12 tasks and three embodiments. Design the collection pipeline, and tell me what makes one demonstration worth keeping.

## Brief answer

Specify a coverage matrix across twelve tasks, three embodiments, environments, objects, operators, and failures before collecting 50,000 hours. Keep demonstrations with synchronized sensors/actions, valid calibration, interpretable task intent, and meaningful behavioral coverage. Raw duration alone rewards redundant or unusable data.

## Detailed answer

**Suggested approach.** Standardize episode IDs, timestamps, camera intrinsics, joint conventions, action units, embodiment version, operator, task instruction, and outcome. Record safety interventions and failed attempts rather than retaining only successes. Validate clock drift, dropped frames, actuator saturation, and corrupted streams at the collection station; quarantine bad episodes before upload.

Schedule collection using downstream policy error clusters and coverage gaps, with minimum quotas for each task–embodiment combination. Separate train/test environments and operators to measure transfer. Retain recovery behavior with an explicit label; distinguish a human rescue from autonomous success. Measure accepted hours per robot-day, diversity, annotation consistency, and improvement on held-out tasks per collection hour. Hardware maintenance, operator fatigue, and consent for recorded people belong in the operating plan. A balanced ten-hour tranche can be more valuable than hundreds of near-identical successful motions.

### Budget accepted coverage, not recording time

There are 36 task–embodiment cells before accounting for objects and environments. Uniformly splitting 50,000 hours would yield about 1,389 hours per cell, but that is only a starting allocation: a delicate insertion task may need more recovery coverage than a repetitive pick-and-place task. Reserve collection capacity for gaps discovered by an initial policy rather than allocating every hour in advance.

An episode should pass three gates. Technical validity checks whether sensor frames and actions are synchronized and interpretable. Behavioral validity checks whether the operator actually performed the labeled task under the stated conditions. Training value checks whether the episode adds a missing state, object, skill transition, or recovery rather than another near-copy. A technically clean failed attempt can be valuable if its outcome and intervention are correctly labeled.

Suppose recording yields 100 hours but calibration and synchronization failures invalidate 20, and long idle periods remove another 15. Report 65 usable hours, not 100 delivered hours. Use this accepted-yield metric to decide whether the next investment should be more operators, better calibration tooling, or a redesigned task protocol.
