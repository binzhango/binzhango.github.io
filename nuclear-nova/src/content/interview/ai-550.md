## Question

Edit rate is the obvious measure of clinician trust. What does it hide, and what would you instrument instead?

## Brief answer

Edit rate mixes trust, style preference, workflow pressure, and unnoticed errors. A clinician may accept an incorrect note because verification is difficult, or heavily edit a correct note to match their preferred style. Measure factual defects and verification effort directly, with attention to what survives into the signed record.

## Detailed answer

Instrument source-span inspection, time to review, sections revisited, accepted/rejected factual suggestions, and corrections after signing under approved privacy controls. Classify edits into factual correction, omission repair, formatting, and stylistic change. Sample accepted notes for independent review so silent errors are visible.

Compare outcomes by specialty and clinician rather than ranking users by raw edit volume. Pair behavioral telemetry with short qualitative feedback about confidence and difficulty. Track unsupported facts, critical omissions, delayed corrections, and patient/encounter mismatches separately. Avoid designing the interface merely to reduce edits: hiding uncertainty or making corrections cumbersome can improve that metric while worsening the product.

### Explain the confounders before using the metric

Edit rate mixes factual corrections, formatting preferences, copied templates, and user behavior. A clinician may rewrite an accurate note into a preferred style, while another accepts a flawed note under time pressure. Therefore, lower edit distance does not automatically mean better clinical quality or lower verification burden.

Label a sample of edits by cause: unsupported assertion, omitted fact, wrong attribution, terminology correction, organization, or stylistic preference. Measure clinically necessary edits separately from optional rewriting. Also audit unchanged passages, because acceptance is not independent evidence of correctness. Without that audit, the metric rewards errors that users fail to notice.

Consider an illustrative comparison: system A requires eight stylistic edits and no factual corrections; system B requires two edits but leaves an unsupported medication assertion untouched. Raw edit rate favors B, while a severity-aware review favors A. The example shows why the metric needs a factual companion, not why editing behavior is useless.

Use paired encounters and, where possible, account for reviewer and specialty differences. Combine critical-error rates, missing-fact rates, time to verified completion, and user feedback. Track edit rate as a workflow signal that prompts investigation. A release decision should require stable or improved factual quality, rather than permitting fewer keystrokes to compensate for more consequential errors.
