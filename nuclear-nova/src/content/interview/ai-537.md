## Question

Serving real-time TTS is a different capacity problem from serving a text LLM. Why, and how do you plan capacity?

## Brief answer

TTS capacity depends on concurrent audio streams, generated audio duration, real-time factor, startup latency, and uninterrupted delivery. A system that generates many audio seconds per second on average can still fail conversational users if first audio is late or playback underruns. Plan for sustained streams and bursty turn starts separately.

## Detailed answer

**Suggested approach.** Measure time-to-first-audio and real-time factor, defined as synthesis wall time divided by produced audio duration, on representative voices, languages, and text lengths. If one worker sustains 20 audio-seconds/second at the required tail latency, 200 simultaneous speaking streams need roughly ten workers before headroom, assuming that throughput holds under concurrent scheduling.

Reserve capacity for startup and cancellation, apply admission control, and use bounded audio buffers. Avoid batching policies that maximize offline throughput while delaying live turns. Model silence, barge-in, network jitter, and voice/model switching in load tests. Track underrun rate, p95 first-audio latency, cancellation waste, and stream completion. Keep a fallback voice or human transfer strategy where continuity matters more than perfect voice fidelity.

### Translate conversational demand into audio work

Suppose 1,000 callers are connected but only 20% are listening to synthesized speech at a given instant. That is 200 active output streams, each consuming approximately one audio-second of production per wall-clock second. At a measured sustainable rate of 20 audio-seconds per second per worker, the lower-bound requirement is ten workers. Connected calls and active synthesis streams are different quantities; using either interchangeably can overestimate or underestimate demand.

Ten is not an adequate deployment recommendation by itself. Correlated turn starts create a startup burst, some voices are slower, long utterances retain state, and losing one worker should not interrupt every assigned call. Load-test the actual scheduling policy at the required first-audio and underrun percentiles, then add capacity for burstiness and failure recovery. Real-time factor below one for an isolated request does not prove that many simultaneous requests meet their deadlines.

Queue admission should consider whether useful playback can still begin on time. A delayed offline narration can wait; an interactive turn several seconds late may already be obsolete. Cancel superseded turns, cap queued audio per call, and keep enough playback buffer to absorb network jitter without making interruption feel sluggish. This balances continuity against responsiveness rather than maximizing raw batch throughput.
