## Question

Why is PDF parsing necessary?

## Brief answer

PDF parsing converts a page-oriented format into usable text and structure for indexing, retrieval, and citation.

## Detailed answer

A PDF can store positioned glyphs rather than paragraphs, contain scanned images, or mix both. Extraction must recover reading order, headings, tables, and page references. Scanned text requires OCR or an image-capable parser. Without this step, a retriever may index disconnected columns or omit the very facts needed to answer a question. Retain page and region provenance so extracted claims can be checked against the original.

PDF is primarily a page-description format. A visually coherent paragraph may be stored as individually positioned characters, and the order of those drawing operations need not match human reading order. A two-column page can therefore yield interleaved sentences if extraction follows the wrong sequence. A scanned page may contain no selectable text at all.

A useful parser reconstructs content and relationships: headings with paragraphs, captions with figures, and table values with their row and column labels. It also preserves page number and bounding boxes so the result can be traced back to the visual source. OCR is needed where text exists only as pixels, but its output can confuse similar characters or omit punctuation.

For retrieval, these errors are not cosmetic. Changing “0.05” to “005” or moving a warning into another section changes the meaning embedded in the index. Test representative page types, compare extracted text with images, and flag low-confidence regions for review. Keep the original PDF available so a downstream answer can be checked against source layout rather than trusting an unverified text conversion.
