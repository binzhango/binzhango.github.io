## Question

Two good clinicians write different notes for the same visit. So how do you evaluate note quality at all?

## Brief answer

Evaluate whether the note faithfully captures clinically relevant facts and supports the intended workflow, not whether it matches one clinician's wording. Use a rubric for omissions, unsupported additions, incorrect attribution, temporality, medication details, and readability. Multiple acceptable notes can differ in organization and style while preserving the same evidence.

## Detailed answer

**Suggested approach.** Have qualified reviewers independently annotate key facts and severity, then adjudicate disagreement and report inter-rater variation. Compare generated notes against the encounter evidence and relevant chart context, not just a single reference note that may itself omit information. Separate factual quality from stylistic preference.

Use blinded paired comparisons, critical-error rates, fact-level precision/recall, and review time. Stratify by specialty, visit complexity, language, and audio quality. Test negation, tentative diagnoses, family history, and corrected statements. Calibrate automated checks against expert labels and retain difficult cases in regression suites. A high lexical-similarity score or low edit distance cannot establish that a note is safe or complete.

### Score clinical facts before surface similarity

Decompose the reference and generated note into assertions with subject, polarity, temporality, and relevant qualifiers. A note that paraphrases “denies shortness of breath” correctly should receive credit despite different wording; a note that drops “denies” must incur a serious factual penalty despite nearly identical words. Compare supported facts, omissions, unsupported additions, and contradictions separately.

Use a rubric reflecting the note's purpose and specialty. A missing incidental conversational detail is not equivalent to a missing medication change or follow-up instruction. Have qualified reviewers annotate severity and adjudicate ambiguous examples, while measuring agreement so rubric problems are visible. More than one note organization can be acceptable.

Evaluate the full workflow with blinded review where practical: can clinicians verify the draft efficiently, locate evidence, and make necessary corrections? Track critical errors per encounter and review time as well as average fact scores. Automatically accepted text can be wrong, and heavily edited text can simply reflect personal style.

Keep evaluation encounters separate from prompt examples and development cases, and stratify by visit length, specialty, noise, language, and speaker complexity. After a model update, compare paired outputs on the same encounters. Inspect regressions directly rather than allowing a better average writing score to offset newly unsupported clinical assertions.
