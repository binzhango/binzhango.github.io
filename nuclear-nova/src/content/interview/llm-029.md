## Question

What’s the core idea of DeepNorm?

## Brief answer

DeepNorm stabilizes deep Transformers by combining a scaled residual connection with depth-dependent initialization.

## Detailed answer

Its sublayer has the form $x_{l+1}=\operatorname{LN}(\alpha x_l+F_l(x_l))$. For a decoder-only stack of $L$ blocks, the paper gives $\alpha=(2L)^{1/4}$ and initialization gain $\beta=(8L)^{-1/4}$ for selected projections. Encoder-decoder stacks use different constants. This is a fourth-root rule in that case, not a generic square-root scaling rule, and it is not simply an alternative normalization statistic.

The purpose of the two coefficients is to control the relative scale of the residual stream and newly added transformations as depth grows. A larger residual multiplier keeps each sublayer's initial perturbation modest relative to the carried state; shrinking selected initial weights also limits the transformed branch. LayerNorm then rescales the combined result. Neither operation alone fully describes the proposed recipe.

For a decoder-only example with $L=32$, $\alpha=(64)^{1/4}\approx2.828$ and $\beta=(256)^{-1/4}=0.25$. These are dimensionless coefficients. They are not a learning-rate schedule and should not be applied indiscriminately to every parameter tensor. The selected projection matrices and initialization conventions matter.

To assess such a change, inspect loss, activation scale, and gradient behavior as depth increases, comparing otherwise controlled configurations. Successful optimization is distinct from useful scaling: a stable very deep model can still be inefficient for a latency-sensitive application. Also distinguish training a model with DeepNorm from altering the residual coefficients of an existing checkpoint, which changes its learned computation and can destroy behavior.
