## Question

Tell me about a time you disagreed with a researcher or tech lead about priorities, and what happened.

## Brief answer

Describe the shared objective, the competing priorities and the evidence each side used. Show how you tested assumptions or clarified the decision owner, then explain the outcome and how you supported it. The strongest answer demonstrates technical judgment and collaboration even when the final choice was not your preference.

## Detailed answer

Response framework: select a real disagreement about research depth, delivery timing, quality thresholds or infrastructure work. Explain why the other person's position was reasonable. Identify what information was missing and whether a small experiment, user evidence or cost estimate could resolve it.

Describe the decision process and what you personally changed after hearing the other view. If uncertainty remained, explain how you bounded the commitment with a milestone or reversible choice. Avoid portraying researchers as impractical or engineering leads as uninterested in quality.

Close with the measured or observable result and a lesson about future prioritization. Be precise about your contribution and do not turn disagreement into a victory story; the aim is better collective decisions under constraints.

An illustrative disagreement is whether to spend another month improving a model benchmark or ship a smaller scoped feature with monitoring. The researcher may be protecting quality, while the lead may be responding to a time-sensitive user need. A useful response identifies a shared success criterion and proposes evidence, such as a limited pilot or a targeted failure analysis, that can change the decision.

For a real story, explain what you initially believed, what the other person contributed and what you changed after hearing it. If the decision remained uncertain, describe a reversible commitment with a review date rather than pretending the debate produced certainty. Supporting the final plan matters even when it is not your preferred option.

Close with an actual outcome and a specific lesson about prioritization. Do not invent a successful metric or portray one discipline as inherently impractical. The question tests whether you can make better collective decisions while preserving a productive working relationship.
