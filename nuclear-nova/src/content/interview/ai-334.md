## Question

Explain the bias-variance trade-off, cross-validation, and the curse of dimensionality.

## Brief answer

Bias is error from restrictive assumptions; variance is sensitivity to the sampled training set. Cross-validation estimates generalization under a chosen split design and helps select model complexity. The curse of dimensionality means data becomes sparse and distance or coverage intuition deteriorates as dimension grows, increasing the data or structural assumptions needed for reliable learning.

## Detailed answer

For squared error under standard assumptions, expected prediction error decomposes into squared bias, variance, and irreducible noise. More flexible models may reduce bias while increasing variance, but the relationship is not a universal monotonic law for every modern training regime.

Choose splits that match deployment: grouped splits for repeated users or patients, chronological splits for forecasting, and nested validation when estimating performance after extensive tuning. Perform imputation, scaling, and feature selection inside each training fold to prevent leakage. In high dimensions, use domain structure, regularization, or learned representations; arbitrary feature deletion can remove useful interactions. Compare learning curves: persistent training error suggests bias, while a large train/validation gap suggests overfitting or distribution mismatch.

### Interpret the three ideas together

A shallow model that misses a curved relationship may have high bias: both training and validation errors remain high. A deep tree that memorizes a small sample may have high variance: training error is low but predictions change substantially with the sample. Cross-validation estimates how choices generalize, provided the folds reproduce the relevant independence assumptions.

Dimensionality makes coverage expensive. A toy grid with ten bins per feature has $10^d$ cells for d features; adding a feature multiplies the possible cells by ten. Real data often lies on lower-dimensional structure, but this example explains why sparse coverage and unreliable distances can appear as features accumulate. Regularization, suitable representations, and more relevant data address different parts of the problem. Use learning curves to distinguish inadequate capacity from inadequate sample size. If validation error stays high despite abundant data and low training error, also investigate a distribution shift or leaked split rather than forcing every failure into the bias–variance story.
