## Question

Why carry lidar, radar and cameras rather than cameras alone? Where would you fuse them?

## Brief answer

Use complementary sensors when their failure modes and information justify the added cost and complexity. Cameras provide rich appearance, lidar provides direct geometric range, and radar provides useful range/velocity information with different environmental sensitivities. Redundancy helps only if calibration, fusion, and common-mode failures are understood.

## Detailed answer

**Suggested approach.** Fuse time-aligned sensor features or object-level estimates at a stage that preserves uncertainty and handles missing modalities. Early fusion can exploit complementary raw evidence but is sensitive to calibration and bandwidth; later fusion is modular but can discard useful cross-sensor detail. A hybrid often combines learned features with track-level consistency checks.

Evaluate detection and planning under glare, darkness, rain, occlusion, sensor obstruction, and calibration drift. Test graceful degradation rather than assuming all sensors always work. Compare safety-relevant performance, latency, power, maintenance, and cost in the intended domain. More sensors do not automatically make the system safer if fusion overtrusts a faulty input or the stack cannot detect disagreement.

### Complementary sensing changes observability

A camera distinguishes appearance and traffic-light state but can struggle with depth under poor texture. Lidar provides geometric range directly, while radar can measure radial velocity and behave differently in darkness or some adverse conditions. Their errors are neither identical nor independent: heavy weather, obstruction, bad calibration, or shared timing failures can affect several modalities.

A feature-level BEV architecture aligns timestamps and coordinate frames, encodes each sensor, projects evidence into a common representation, and includes validity/uncertainty masks before detection and tracking. A parallel object-level consistency check can flag disagreement rather than averaging it away. Early fusion offers richer cross-modal interactions but tighter synchronization and compute demands; late fusion is easier to modularize but can lose information that resolves ambiguity. Test a blocked camera, radar ghost, missing lidar channel, and time offset explicitly. The choice should be justified by task-relevant performance and degraded behavior under the operating domain, including cost and maintenance, rather than sensor count alone.
