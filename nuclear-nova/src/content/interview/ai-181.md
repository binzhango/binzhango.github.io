## Question

An enterprise customer says: “We want AI to automate our claims processing.” You're the engineer in the room. What do the first two weeks look like?

## Brief answer

Spend the first two weeks defining the claims workflow, evidence requirements and human decision boundaries before automating decisions. Map the current process, collect representative cases with appropriate access and build a narrow assisted prototype. Claims can involve consequential outcomes, so success must include error cost, review workload and traceability rather than only model extraction accuracy.

## Detailed answer

In week one, interview operators and decision owners, document input formats and exceptions, and identify the authoritative policy rules. Separate document ingestion, extraction, evidence gathering, recommendation and final adjudication. Build a labeled sample including missing documents, contradictory evidence and unusual claims; do not start with only easy completed cases.

In week two, prototype one bounded stage, such as extracting fields and flagging missing evidence, with source citations and human correction. Compare against the current process on turnaround time, error severity and review effort. Keep final decisions with authorized staff until the organization validates any broader automation.

Deliver a data map, baseline, evaluation set, prototype and rollout proposal with owners. Security, retention and applicable policy/legal requirements need the customer's specialists; do not infer compliance from a model's output. Stop or narrow the project if necessary evidence or decision accountability is unavailable.

Choose a narrow pilot whose output can be verified against source documents. Extracting claim identifiers, dates and missing attachments is easier to audit than deciding final eligibility. Keep policy interpretation, evidence gathering and adjudication as separate stages so errors can be localized and authorized staff retain the decision boundary.

Build the evaluation sample from the real distribution, including denied, appealed, incomplete and unusual claims. Completed easy cases alone will overstate automation potential. Measure field errors, unsupported recommendations, review time and the cost of a wrong outcome. A model that reduces extraction time but increases reviewer verification effort may not improve the process.

By the end of two weeks, the deliverable should include a working assisted path, error analysis, access/retention map and a phased rollout proposal with explicit owners. State what evidence would justify expanding automation and what would stop it. The customer's operational and specialist reviewers must define applicable decision and compliance requirements; the engineering prototype should make those controls implementable rather than claim that model confidence establishes compliance.
