## Question

A model passes bit-exact against the functional simulator on one chip but produces wrong output at rack scale. How do you find it?

## Brief answer

Reduce the failing distributed execution to the smallest topology and earliest divergent tensor. A correct single-chip functional simulation does not cover packet routing, synchronization, distributed layouts, timing assumptions, or hardware faults. Compare against both numerical and communication references, and preserve the exact program, inputs, topology, and firmware versions for reproduction.

## Detailed answer

Scale from one chip to a pair, then groups and racks, using known patterns before the full model. Check shard placement, transpose/layout conventions, collective membership, packet sequence numbers, buffer lifetimes, and stale state across requests. Insert checksums or sampled tensor comparisons at stage boundaries to binary-search the first corruption.

Distinguish deterministic arithmetic differences from intermittent transport or race failures. Test different link routes, clock conditions, message sizes, and concurrent traffic. A functional simulator may ignore timing, so use a cycle-aware simulator or hardware traces for schedule hazards. If the failure follows a physical component, quarantine it; if it follows a logical partition, inspect the compiler/runtime mapping. Do not “fix” unexplained corruption by loosening output tolerances.

### Find the earliest incorrect boundary

Use inputs with recognizable patterns—rank IDs, ramps, and sparse impulses—to reveal shard permutations and dropped segments. Compare checksums or sampled values before and after each inter-chip transfer. If stage 7 is correct and stage 8 diverges, inspect that boundary before examining the whole model. Reproduce at the smallest device group that still fails.

A deterministic wrong permutation suggests layout or collective membership; intermittent corruption under load suggests lifetime, synchronization, transport, or stale-buffer issues. Move the same logical partition to different physical links and devices to see whether the failure follows hardware or mapping. Functional bit-exact simulation may omit timing and contention, so passing it does not validate a multi-chip schedule. Check sequence numbers, transfer sizes, clock alignment, and buffer reuse after cancellation. Preserve the failing program, input, topology, and firmware versions. A tolerance increase is appropriate only after explaining a legitimate numerical difference, never as a way to hide unexplained rack-scale corruption.
