## Question

SQL: write a query to find the top five pairs of products most frequently purchased together.

## Brief answer

Deduplicate each order's product IDs, self-join on the same order using a strict product ordering, count orders per pair, then take the five largest counts. This counts co-purchases once per basket regardless of quantity and avoids counting both `(A,B)` and `(B,A)`.

## Detailed answer

Assume `order_items(order_id, product_id, quantity)` and that canceled/returned orders have already been excluded by a join to the relevant order status table.

```sql
WITH basket AS (
  SELECT DISTINCT order_id, product_id
  FROM order_items
  WHERE quantity > 0 AND product_id IS NOT NULL
), pairs AS (
  SELECT a.product_id AS first_product,
         b.product_id AS second_product,
         COUNT(*) AS orders_together
  FROM basket a
  JOIN basket b
    ON a.order_id = b.order_id
   AND a.product_id < b.product_id
  GROUP BY a.product_id, b.product_id
)
SELECT first_product, second_product, orders_together
FROM pairs
ORDER BY orders_together DESC, first_product, second_product
LIMIT 5;
```

The ordering gives deterministic ties. A basket with $m$ unique products emits $m(m-1)/2$ pairs, so unusually large wholesale baskets can dominate both compute and counts. Clarify whether the business wants raw frequency, unique users or lift relative to product popularity; these are different metrics. Indexing `(order_id, product_id)` helps the join but cannot eliminate the inherent number of pairs.

Deduplication occurs before the self-join so a basket containing three units of A and two rows for B still contributes one occurrence of pair `(A,B)`. The strict ordering `a.product_id < b.product_id` both excludes self-pairs and avoids symmetric duplicates. Grouping then counts baskets containing each pair, not purchased units.

For baskets `{A,B,C}` and `{A,B}`, the emitted pairs are AB, AC and BC from the first, plus AB from the second. AB therefore has count two; AC and BC have one. This miniature case catches both missing deduplication and double counting. Add tests for single-product orders, returns, null products and tied counts.

At scale, a very large basket generates quadratic pair work, so understand whether such baskets are valid or need a business-defined cap. Raw co-purchase frequency favors popular products. If the goal is recommendations, compare lift or conditional probability and support thresholds so a rare accidental pair does not outrank a reliable association. Those are different outputs and should not be silently substituted for the requested top-five frequency query.
