## Question

What is KL divergence?

## Brief answer

KL divergence measures how one probability distribution differs from another: $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q)=\sum_xp(x)\log[p(x)/q(x)]$.

## Detailed answer

It is nonnegative and equals zero when the distributions agree almost everywhere, but it is asymmetric and is not a distance metric. Terms with $p(x)=0$ contribute zero; if $p(x)>0$ where $q(x)=0$, the divergence is infinite. With natural logs its unit is nats. In distillation, the direction and temperature specify which mismatches receive the greatest penalty.

An intuitive interpretation is the extra expected log loss incurred by using $q$ when outcomes follow $p$. Let $p=(0.5,0.5)$ and $q=(0.9,0.1)$. Then

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q)=\tfrac12\log(0.5/0.9)+\tfrac12\log(0.5/0.1)\approx0.511\ \text{nats}.
$$

Reversing the distributions gives approximately 0.368 nats, demonstrating asymmetry. The first direction strongly objects that $q$ assigns only 0.1 probability to an event that occurs half the time under $p$.

For categorical distillation, take the teacher as $p$ and student as $q$ when the intent is to train the student to match the teacher's distribution. A temperature changes both distributions and the scale of gradients, so report it with the objective. In reinforcement-learning-style regularization, the direction of a policy/reference KL changes which deviations are penalized.

Compute with stable log probabilities and valid supports. Adding an arbitrary epsilon changes the mathematical objective and should be a deliberate numerical policy. KL describes distributions, not whether either distribution represents the real world accurately; perfectly copying a mistaken teacher can yield zero KL while retaining its mistakes.
