## Question

How do traditional table-extraction methods work?

## Brief answer

Traditional table extraction uses ruling lines, intersections, whitespace, and alignment heuristics to infer cells and their text.

## Detailed answer

Line-based methods work well for clearly bordered tables; text-based methods infer columns and rows from aligned words in borderless layouts. They can be fast and inspectable, but depend on document conventions and tuned tolerances. Merged cells, irregular spacing, skew, and tables spanning pages break simple assumptions. Use visual debugging and structural checks rather than trusting an extraction merely because it returned rows.

A line-based method first collects horizontal and vertical ruling segments, snaps nearby coordinates according to tolerances, finds intersections, and constructs candidate rectangular cells. Text whose coordinates fall inside each cell is assigned to it. This works well when borders accurately describe the table, but decorative lines or broken borders can create extra or missing cells.

A text-based method instead clusters words into rows and identifies repeated horizontal alignments as column boundaries. It can recover borderless tables, but wrapped labels and varying numeric widths make alignment ambiguous. Whitespace is evidence about layout, not a guaranteed delimiter between semantic columns.

Tune on actual page images. If two nearby columns merge, inspect their coordinate gap and the joining tolerance. If a header becomes several rows, inspect line spacing and multi-line cells. Crop the relevant region to reduce interference from surrounding text. Validate the resulting row/column relationships and numeric fields against the source. Heuristics are useful because they are fast and explainable, but document-specific tuning should not be mistaken for a universally reliable table parser.
