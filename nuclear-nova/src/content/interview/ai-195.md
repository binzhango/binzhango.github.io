## Question

Derive the gradient of cross-entropy loss with softmax inputs, and explain why we fuse them numerically.

## Brief answer

For softmax probabilities p and a normalized target distribution y, the gradient of cross-entropy with respect to each logit is `p - y`. Computing log-softmax and cross-entropy together avoids forming tiny probabilities and then taking their logarithms. The derivation depends on differentiating logits, not treating already-softmaxed probabilities as independent inputs.

## Detailed answer

Let $p_i=e^{z_i}/\sum_j e^{z_j}$ and $L=-\sum_i y_i\log p_i$, with $\sum_i y_i=1$. Rewrite the loss as $L=\log\sum_j e^{z_j}-\sum_i y_i z_i$. Differentiating gives $\partial L/\partial z_k=p_k-y_k$.

For stability, let $m=\max_j z_j$ and compute $\log\sum_j e^{z_j}=m+\log\sum_j e^{z_j-m}$. The loss remains finite even when a naive softmax would underflow a low-probability target to zero. Framework cross-entropy functions typically expect raw logits; applying softmax first changes the computation and harms stability.

Check a two-class example with equal logits and target class zero: p is `(0.5,0.5)` and the gradient is `(-0.5,0.5)`. The components sum to zero because adding a constant to every logit leaves probabilities unchanged. Batch averaging adds a factor of `1 / batch_size`; class weights and unnormalized targets alter the formula.

The softmax Jacobian is $\partial p_i/\partial z_k=p_i(\delta_{ik}-p_k)$. Combining it with $\partial L/\partial p_i=-y_i/p_i$ gives $-y_k+p_k\sum_i y_i$, which reduces to $p_k-y_k$ for normalized targets. This also shows exactly why unnormalized target weights change the gradient.

With logits `[1000,0]` and the second class correct, the true loss is close to 1000. A naive exponentiation overflows on the first logit, while a separately rounded softmax may assign the target probability zero and produce infinite loss. Log-sum-exp keeps the computation finite by subtracting the maximum before exponentiation.

For a numerical check, perturb one logit by a small positive and negative amount and compare the central finite difference with the analytic gradient. Test moderate logits for the derivative check and extreme logits for stability. Account for reduction conventions: summing examples and averaging examples differ by batch size, and masking changes the effective denominator.
