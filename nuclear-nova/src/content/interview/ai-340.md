## Question

What is Maximum Likelihood Estimation and how does it differ from Bayesian inference?

## Brief answer

Maximum likelihood chooses parameters that maximize the probability of the observed data under a model. Bayesian inference combines that likelihood with a prior to obtain a posterior distribution over parameters. MLE produces a point estimate by default; Bayesian inference explicitly represents parameter uncertainty, though both depend on model assumptions and can be badly miscalibrated under misspecification.

## Detailed answer

For independent observations, $\hat\theta_{MLE}=\arg\max_\theta\sum_i\log p(x_i\mid\theta)$. Bayes' rule gives $p(\theta\mid D)\propto p(D\mid\theta)p(\theta)$. A maximum a posteriori estimate is a posterior mode, not the entire posterior; regularized optimization often corresponds to a particular prior.

For a Bernoulli probability, MLE is successes divided by trials. A Beta prior yields a Beta posterior, allowing a posterior mean and credible interval even with few observations. A frequentist confidence interval and a Bayesian credible interval have different interpretations. Choose based on the decision, uncertainty requirements, prior information, and computation available; Bayesian does not mean assumption-free, and MLE does not mean uncertainty cannot be estimated.

### Work through a coin example

After eight heads in ten independent flips, the Bernoulli MLE is 0.8. With a uniform Beta(1,1) prior, the posterior is Beta(9,3), whose mean is 9/12=0.75 and mode is 8/10=0.8. The posterior mean differs because it averages over uncertainty rather than selecting the most likely parameter value. A stronger prior would influence this small dataset more.

If all ten flips are heads, MLE is 1, while the same posterior mean is 11/12. This avoids claiming absolute certainty from a small sample, though the conclusion depends on the chosen prior and model. Bayesian prediction integrates over the posterior; plug-in MLE prediction uses the fitted parameter, with uncertainty estimated separately when needed. As data grows under regular conditions, likelihood information often dominates a weak prior. Neither approach repairs a wrong independence assumption or biased sampling: modeling how observations were collected matters before choosing an estimator.
