## Question

Compare BPE, WordPiece and Unigram tokenization. Why is `tokenizers` written in Rust, and what tokenizer bugs bite people in practice?

## Brief answer

BPE learns frequent adjacent-symbol merges; WordPiece builds a subword vocabulary using a likelihood-oriented selection rule and commonly uses longest-match segmentation; Unigram begins with a candidate vocabulary and prunes it under a probabilistic model. Runtime tokenization depends on normalization and pre-tokenization too. Rust supports fast, parallel, memory-safe implementation, but does not by itself guarantee tokenization correctness.

## Detailed answer

BPE produces deterministic segmentation for fixed merges, while Unigram can support sampled segmentations useful for regularization. WordPiece commonly uses continuation markers and an unknown-token fallback, though details vary by implementation. Byte-level schemes improve coverage but can fragment unfamiliar scripts.

Common bugs include train/serve normalization differences, duplicated special tokens, changed added-token IDs, incorrect offset mappings after Unicode normalization, truncating control markers, and padding on the wrong side for a particular inference path. Compare exact token IDs and round-trip behavior on multilingual text, emoji, whitespace, code, and special-token literals. Pin tokenizer files with the model rather than assuming a similarly named tokenizer is compatible.

### The algorithms choose pieces differently

BPE begins with small units and repeatedly merges frequent adjacent pairs; its learned merge order determines segmentation. WordPiece uses a learned subword vocabulary and typically greedy longest-match segmentation with continuation conventions. Unigram begins with candidate pieces and learns a probabilistic vocabulary, choosing a segmentation with high likelihood and optionally sampling alternatives during training. Normalization and pre-tokenization are part of all three pipelines and can matter as much as the core algorithm.

Rust reduces per-character interpreter overhead and supports efficient parallel tokenization with language bindings, which matters when tokenization otherwise starves training or serving. It does not prevent configuration mistakes. A visually identical string may contain different Unicode sequences; normalization can also change offsets used for span labels. Test exact IDs, offsets, special-token handling, and decoding on adversarial whitespace and multilingual examples. Keep training and serving assets identical. Replacing a tokenizer without adapting model embeddings changes the meaning of token IDs and is a model compatibility break, not a harmless preprocessing swap.
