## Question

Tell me about a time you disagreed with your team's technical direction. What did you do? (Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit)

## Brief answer

Response framework: describe a real disagreement about a technical choice, the shared objective, and the evidence behind your concern. Explain how you challenged the idea respectfully, helped the team make a decision, and supported execution once the decision was made. Commitment does not mean hiding new evidence or ignoring serious safety and integrity issues.

## Detailed answer

Choose a disagreement with meaningful alternatives, such as operational complexity versus delivery speed. State what you knew, what the other side reasonably valued, and how you tested assumptions through a prototype, benchmark, or written trade-off. Distinguish your personal contribution from the team's decision.

After the decision, explain the actions you took to make the chosen direction succeed and the conditions agreed for reconsideration. Use the actual outcome, including if the other option proved better than yours. End with what changed in your approach to disagreement. Avoid portraying colleagues as irrational or claiming that being right was more important than delivering the shared goal.

### Show both the challenge and the commitment

**Illustrative scenario:** one engineer favors a custom retrieval service for flexibility while the team favors a managed index to meet a deadline. A strong response explains why each option fits a legitimate objective, presents a small benchmark or cost model, and records the decision criteria. After the team chooses the managed option, the engineer helps ship it and defines measurable conditions for revisiting the choice.

Use your real example to explain how you raised concerns without turning disagreement into a personal contest. State what evidence would have changed your own position, who had decision authority, and what you did after the decision. Commitment does not require hiding new safety or correctness evidence; explain how you would escalate a material change. Conclude with the observed outcome and a specific lesson about decision quality or communication, rather than claiming that your proposal was inevitably superior.
