## Question

When would you choose Q-learning over policy gradients, and vice versa?

## Brief answer

Choose Q-learning when actions are discrete or can be efficiently maximized and off-policy reuse is valuable. Choose policy gradients when actions are continuous, stochastic policies are important or direct policy optimization is easier than maximizing a learned Q function. Actor–critic methods combine both ideas, so the choice is not a strict binary.

## Detailed answer

Q-learning estimates action values using a bootstrapped target such as $r+\gamma\max_a Q(s',a)$. Replay can improve sample efficiency, but function approximation, off-policy data and bootstrapping can create instability. Large or continuous action spaces make the maximization difficult.

Policy gradients optimize expected return through gradients of action log-probabilities weighted by return or advantage. They naturally handle parameterized continuous distributions, but gradient variance and data requirements can be substantial. Baselines, advantage estimation and constrained updates improve stability.

Compare exploration, reward sparsity, safety constraints, environment cost and action structure. If collecting experience is expensive, sample efficiency matters more than a small implementation difference. If actions are generated sequences, policy-gradient-style objectives may fit naturally, but reward quality and credit assignment remain central challenges. Validate against a simpler control or bandit baseline where appropriate.

For a small discrete control problem, Q-learning can store or approximate the value of each action and choose the maximum efficiently. Reusing past transitions through replay can be valuable when environment interaction is expensive. However, an inaccurate maximum can amplify overestimation, and combining bootstrapping with off-policy function approximation requires stabilization and careful evaluation.

For continuous control, a policy can directly output parameters of a distribution over actions, avoiding a difficult maximization over every possible real-valued action. Policy gradients use sampled returns or advantages to adjust that distribution, but estimates can be noisy. Actor–critic methods learn a value estimate to reduce variance while retaining a parameterized policy.

Choose using action structure, data availability, exploration needs and the cost of failure. Offline logged data creates additional coverage problems for either family: optimizing actions absent from the data can exploit estimation errors. Compare learning curves in environment steps and wall time, and evaluate under the same reward and safety constraints. Algorithm names alone do not settle the design; reward quality and trustworthy evaluation often dominate the outcome.
