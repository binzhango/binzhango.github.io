## Question

How should data volume, training compute and serving requirements determine model size?

## Brief answer

Choose model size jointly with available data, training compute, and deployment requirements.

## Detailed answer

For dense Transformer pretraining, $C\approx6ND$ is a rough FLOP estimate using parameter count $N$ and training tokens $D$; it omits important details such as long-context attention and exact architecture. Compute-optimal training and inference-efficient deployment can favor different choices. A smaller model trained longer may be useful when serving costs dominate. Treat scaling-law results as experimental guidance under their assumptions, not universal token-per-parameter rules.

The approximation makes the tradeoff concrete. For $N=7\times10^9$ parameters and $D=10^{12}$ training tokens, $6ND\approx4.2\times10^{22}$ FLOPs. At a sustained aggregate throughput of $10^{15}$ FLOPs per second, the idealized compute time is $4.2\times10^7$ seconds, about 486 days. That throughput is an illustrative sustained value, not a GPU specification or a measured training result; overhead and architecture details can increase time.

For a fixed training-compute budget, choosing a larger model leaves fewer tokens under this approximation. Too little data can undertrain the model relative to a smaller alternative. But deployment changes the objective: a heavily used service may justify training a smaller model on more tokens to reduce repeated inference costs.

Estimate both one-time training and lifetime serving under expected traffic. Consider required context length, KV memory, concurrency, and latency, not just parameter FLOPs. Use smaller-scale experiments to estimate whether more data or capacity improves the target tasks. Published scaling relationships are useful guides within their experimental conditions, not universal prescriptions for every domain, architecture, or post-training workflow.
