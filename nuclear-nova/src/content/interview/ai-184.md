## Question

Tell me about a time you made a mistake.

## Brief answer

Describe a real mistake, its impact and your response. Make clear what you controlled, how you communicated the problem and what changed afterward. The answer should demonstrate accountability and improved judgment, not shift blame or disguise a strength as a failure.

## Detailed answer

Response framework: give enough context to understand the decision, then identify the mistaken assumption or action. Explain how the problem surfaced and how you limited further damage. Include who needed to know and what you told them, especially if the news was uncomfortable.

Describe the correction and its cost honestly. Then identify a durable change—such as a verification step, an ownership agreement or a better feedback loop—and explain whether it actually prevented recurrence. Avoid claiming that one mistake permanently eliminated an entire class of failures.

Keep confidential details generalized and do not invent metrics. A small but consequential example you can discuss deeply is stronger than a dramatic story with unclear personal responsibility.

An illustrative mistake could be deploying a configuration change without checking its effect on a low-volume workflow. The useful structure is the assumption, the missed validation, the observed impact, the containment and the process change. This is only an example of a story shape; use a real incident rather than adopting it as personal history.

Be specific about your agency. “The release failed” hides whether you made the decision, implemented it or inherited the issue. Explain what you should have done with the information available then, without pretending you could predict everything in hindsight. Acknowledge warnings or uncertainty that you discounted.

The response should show repair as well as reflection: informing affected people, restoring service or correcting the artifact, and checking whether the remedy worked. Finish with a durable change such as a targeted preflight test or clearer ownership. Avoid claiming the lesson guarantees no future mistakes; demonstrate that it improved how you detect and contain similar risks.
