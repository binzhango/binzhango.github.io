## Question

What is the relationship between cross-entropy, KL divergence and perplexity, and why is cross-entropy the training loss for language models?

## Brief answer

Cross-entropy equals the target distribution's entropy plus its KL divergence from the model distribution. Minimizing cross-entropy therefore minimizes KL when the target distribution is fixed. Perplexity is the exponential of average token cross-entropy with natural logs. Next-token cross-entropy is maximum-likelihood training for an autoregressive language model.

## Detailed answer

For target distribution p and prediction q,

$$H(p,q)=-\sum_xp(x)\log q(x)=H(p)+D_{KL}(p\Vert q).$$

An observed next-token label gives a one-hot target, so the sample loss is $-\log q(y)$. Summing over positions is the negative log-likelihood of the sequence under the autoregressive factorization. Gradients reward probability on observed tokens while preserving a normalized distribution over alternatives.

If mean loss is $\log 4$, perplexity is 4. This is an effective uncertainty measure, not literally a claim that the model always considers exactly four equally likely tokens. Compare perplexity only with compatible tokenization, data and masking conventions. Averaging sequence perplexities differs from exponentiating the token-weighted mean loss.

Lower cross-entropy does not directly guarantee better factuality, instruction following or task success. It optimizes predictive fit to the training distribution, including any undesirable patterns that distribution contains.

For two observed tokens assigned probabilities 0.5 and 0.25, mean negative log-likelihood is $[-\log0.5-\log0.25]/2=\log\sqrt8$. Perplexity is therefore $\sqrt8\approx2.828$, the reciprocal geometric mean of the assigned probabilities. Averaging the individual perplexities would give $(2+4)/2=3$, a different quantity.

The entropy term $H(p)$ in the decomposition is fixed when the target distribution is fixed, so reducing cross-entropy reduces divergence from that target. For a sampled one-hot label, the empirical loss is simple to compute even though the true language distribution is unknown. Training aggregates many such samples to estimate predictive performance.

Tokenization changes the unit of prediction: splitting a word into more pieces changes both token count and per-token loss, so perplexities across incompatible tokenizers are not directly comparable. Also specify which tokens are masked in chat training. Low loss on copied user text can conceal poor assistant-response modeling if the evaluation includes the wrong positions.
