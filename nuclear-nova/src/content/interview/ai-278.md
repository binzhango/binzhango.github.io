## Question

Walk me through why you would disaggregate prefill and decode onto separate machines, as Mooncake does. What does that buy you and what does it cost?

## Brief answer

Prefill processes many prompt tokens in parallel and is often compute-heavy; decode repeatedly generates small token batches and is often limited by memory traffic or cache capacity. Separating them allows different hardware and scheduling policies and prevents a long prompt from stalling ongoing generation. The price is KV transfer, duplicated model capacity, and distributed coordination.

## Detailed answer

Route requests to a prefill pool using prompt length and reusable-prefix locations. After prefill, transfer the compatible KV state to a decode worker and continue generation. Transfer bytes, network bandwidth, serialization, and cache layout determine whether this wins; a slow network can consume the entire latency benefit.

Use independent queue limits and scaling signals: prompt-token throughput and first-token deadlines for prefill; cache occupancy and inter-token deadlines for decode. Handle transfer failure, cancellation, model-version mismatches, and orphaned state explicitly. Keep admission control when demand exceeds capacity rather than allowing unbounded queues. Mooncake organizes serving around disaggregated KV storage and scheduling; its reported gains depend on workload and baseline.

### When the network erases the benefit

A 4 GiB KV state takes at least about 172 ms to transfer over a hypothetical usable 25 GB/s link: divide 4 × 2³⁰ bytes by 25 × 10⁹ bytes/s. Protocol overhead, contention, and layout conversion add to this lower bound. If separating the workloads saves only 100 ms, remote transfer cannot pay for itself unless the state is already colocated or reused.

The scheduler therefore needs cache-location and transfer-cost awareness. It can select a decode worker near the prefill result, reserve decode capacity before expensive prefill begins, and cancel reservations when clients leave. Disaggregation is especially attractive when long prefills disrupt many latency-sensitive decodes, or when prompt and output demand scale differently. A small, low-concurrency service may do better with chunked prefill on a shared pool because it avoids transfer and duplicated capacity. Compare the entire queue–prefill–transfer–decode path under realistic bursts.
