## Question

Tell me about a time a technical decision you championed turned out to be wrong. What happened, and what did you change afterward?

## Brief answer

Response framework: choose a real decision you advocated, explain why it seemed reasonable with the evidence then available, and identify the evidence that showed it was wrong. Describe how you took responsibility, reduced the impact, and changed the decision process afterward. The strongest answer demonstrates learning without rewriting history or blaming others.

## Detailed answer

Structure the story around the decision rather than a long project chronology. State the goal, constraints, alternatives, your recommendation, and the assumptions that proved false. Distinguish an unlucky outcome from a flawed process: a reasonable experiment can fail, while ignoring disconfirming evidence is a different issue.

Explain what you personally did to detect and acknowledge the mistake, who was affected, and how you repaired or reversed it. Use real metrics where available and describe uncertainty honestly. End with a concrete change such as earlier load testing, a reversible rollout, or explicit kill criteria, then give evidence that the change influenced later work. Avoid a disguised success story where the only mistake was caring too much.

### A credible response has a falsifiable turning point

**Illustrative example, not personal history:** an engineer chose a shared service to avoid duplicated logic, then load tests revealed that its central queue made unrelated customers wait behind large jobs. A useful account would explain the original traffic assumption, the trace that disproved it, the affected users, and the decision to isolate workloads or roll back.

In your own answer, identify the evidence you initially had and why the choice was reasonable or flawed at that time. State exactly when you changed your mind and how you communicated it. “I learned to test more” is too vague; describe a changed mechanism such as a workload-specific load-test gate or a documented rollback threshold. Be ready to discuss alternatives and residual costs. Ownership means acknowledging your contribution to the mistake and recovery without claiming that every uncertain decision should have been predictable.
