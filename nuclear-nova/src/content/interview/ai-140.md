## Question

How do scaling laws influence the safety evaluation of large models?

## Brief answer

Scaling laws can forecast average loss or capability trends, but they do not establish safety at the next scale. Rare harmful behaviors, tool use and thresholded benchmarks can change differently from aggregate loss. Use smaller runs to prioritize tests, then evaluate the actual larger model under realistic access and scaffolding conditions before release.

## Detailed answer

A compute-optimal training law helps choose parameter count and data budget; that objective is distinct from measuring dangerous capabilities. Chinchilla concerns training allocation, not a theorem that safety improves or degrades smoothly with scale.

Build evaluation curves across checkpoints and sizes using continuous metrics where possible, with confidence intervals and stable prompts. A score can appear to “emerge” because a benchmark converts gradual improvement into pass/fail. Also test whether longer reasoning budgets, tools or repeated attempts unlock capability that a single-turn benchmark misses.

Set release gates based on observed risk and mitigation effectiveness, not extrapolated average performance. Track distribution shifts and measurement ceilings: a saturated benchmark gives little information about the next model. Reserve independent red-team cases and holdouts so repeated scaling decisions do not overfit the same safety suite.

Suppose a task requires ten steps and each step's success probability improves gradually with scale. Whole-task success can rise sharply because all steps must work, even though the underlying change is smooth. A binary benchmark may therefore make a capability look suddenly emergent. This is a measurement issue to investigate, not a reason to dismiss a new practical capability.

Evaluate the deployment's actual affordances: tools, memory, repeated attempts and longer inference budgets can expose abilities that a short unassisted benchmark misses. Conversely, a capability demonstrated in an unconstrained sandbox may be hard to exercise through a restricted product interface. Separate model capability from reachable application risk.

Scaling forecasts help allocate evaluation effort and identify where existing tests may saturate. They cannot substitute for testing the released checkpoint. Use stronger holdouts, adaptive red teaming and intervention tests as performance rises, and retain evidence about uncertainty. A smoothly improving average loss provides little assurance about rare high-impact failures or whether a particular mitigation still works at the new capability level.
