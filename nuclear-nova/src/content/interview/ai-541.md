## Question

Design the dubbing pipeline: an English video becomes Spanish, same speakers, same timing.

## Brief answer

Separate transcription, speaker identity, translation, timing adaptation, synthesis, and mixing, while carrying time-aligned provenance through the pipeline. Preserve meaning and speaker intent before optimizing exact duration. Some utterances cannot fit the original timing naturally without rewriting, and those cases need controlled adaptation or review.

## Detailed answer

**Suggested approach.** Diarize the source, align words and turns, identify names and terminology, and translate with surrounding context. Produce target-language segments with duration targets and pronunciation guidance. Synthesize only with authorized voices, then adjust phrasing or bounded speaking rate before applying mild time stretching. Keep music and effects separate where source stems allow.

Review semantic fidelity, voice consistency, lip/timing alignment, overlaps, and loudness. Test code-switching, jokes, fast dialogue, off-screen speech, and imperfect speaker separation. Store source-to-target segment links so corrections regenerate only affected regions. Measure human-rated translation accuracy and naturalness alongside timing error. A same-duration waveform is not success if it changes meaning or makes speech unintelligible.

### Carry constraints without sacrificing the message

Give every source segment a speaker ID, start/end time, transcript, translation, and approved target voice. Translate related turns together so pronouns, jokes, and terminology remain consistent, then align the translated segments back to timing windows. Independent sentence translation can preserve individual words while losing who a later sentence refers to.

Consider an English line occupying two seconds whose natural Spanish translation takes three. First try an idiomatic shorter translation that preserves the proposition and tone. Next use a modest speaking-rate adjustment if it remains intelligible. If the line still cannot fit, flag the segment for editorial adjustment rather than compressing speech until it sounds unnatural or deleting meaningful qualifications. Exact lip synchronization may require a separate visual workflow and approval; matching audio duration alone does not achieve it.

Regenerate locally when a term changes, but include neighboring context needed for voice continuity and prosody. Check the resulting boundaries for abrupt timbre, loudness, or timing shifts. Overlapping speakers need separate tracks so one correction does not erase the other's speech.

The acceptance process should use bilingual reviewers for semantic fidelity and listeners for naturalness. Automated alignment error can prioritize problematic segments, but a perfectly aligned mistranslation remains a failed output. Keep authorized voice use and the source-to-target edit history attached to the deliverable.
