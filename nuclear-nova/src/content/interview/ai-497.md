## Question

Your agent passes 92% of eval tasks. Why might that number be misleading, and what would you measure instead?

## Brief answer

A 92% task pass rate can hide an unrepresentative test set, unequal task severity, generous retries, and failures that matter much more than successes. It can also combine tool outages with reasoning errors or count superficial answer quality as completed business work. Inspect the denominator and failure distribution before trusting the number.

## Detailed answer

Report end-to-end completion at a fixed budget, permission violations, unsupported confirmations, recovery rate, latency, cost, and human intervention. Break results down by task type, workflow length, customer cohort, and risk. Include repeated runs so a task that succeeds once in ten attempts is not treated as reliably solved.

For consequential workflows, track invariant violations separately with strict release gates. Measure calibration: does the agent recognize when to abstain or escalate? Compare offline scores with live resolution and downstream correction rates. Use confidence intervals and coverage statements, and preserve failures for review. The useful question is whether the system is dependable for the proposed deployment scope, not whether 92 sounds high.

### Inspect what the remaining eight percent contains

Suppose 920 of 1,000 tasks pass. If the other 80 are polite refusals on unsupported requests, the deployment implications differ greatly from 80 unauthorized payments. The aggregate also changes meaning if failed tool calls are excluded, if easy intents dominate the sample, or if a “pass” means only that a judge liked the final wording.

Under a simple independent Bernoulli approximation, a 92% rate on 1,000 tasks has a standard error of about 0.86 percentage points. That statistical uncertainty says nothing about whether the sample represents future customers. A narrow test set can estimate the wrong population very precisely.

Create a dashboard with task completion at fixed attempts, severe invariant violations, appropriate abstention, recovery from unknown outcomes, and cost through resolution. Add per-intent and per-cohort views with sample sizes. Then inspect whether human reviewers can identify and correct the failures efficiently. The launch decision should concern the supported task scope and consequences of error, not a universal target percentage detached from those conditions.
