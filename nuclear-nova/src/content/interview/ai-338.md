## Question

How do you handle data imbalance, collinearity, feature selection and regularization?

## Brief answer

Handle each problem according to its mechanism: imbalance affects sampling and decision thresholds, collinearity affects coefficient stability, feature selection affects complexity and leakage, and regularization constrains fitting. Start with a valid split and a simple baseline, then test interventions on deployment-representative metrics. Applying all techniques blindly can worsen calibration and discard useful signal.

## Detailed answer

For imbalance, use class-weighted loss or resampling inside training folds and evaluate precision-recall at realistic prevalence. Adjust thresholds to costs; oversampling is not a substitute for gathering rare positive examples. For collinearity, inspect redundant features and use ridge or elastic net when stable coefficients matter; prediction can remain good despite unstable individual coefficients.

Fit feature selectors only on training data, retaining domain-critical variables and checking stability across folds. Tune regularization strength alongside model capacity using grouped or temporal validation when needed. Compare calibration, subgroup performance, and operational cost. Investigate missingness and leakage first: a highly predictive feature recorded after the outcome is an invalid shortcut, regardless of regularization.

### Build a leakage-safe experiment

Consider rare churn prediction with many correlated billing features. Split customers by time first, then fit imputation, scaling, feature selection, and any resampling within each training fold. A cancellation timestamp recorded after churn is leakage and must be removed, even if regularization gives it a small coefficient. Duplicating rare positives before splitting can place copies in both train and validation and inflate performance.

Use a simple regularized baseline to identify whether complexity adds value. Class weighting changes the optimization's emphasis; threshold selection changes the decision rule; probability calibration addresses whether predicted probabilities match observed rates. These are distinct steps. Correlated predictors may not hurt a tree's predictions but can distort importance rankings or destabilize linear coefficients. Evaluate selected features across folds and prefer stable, available-at-serving-time inputs. Report the review workload or cost at the chosen threshold alongside precision, recall, and calibration, so the solution addresses the actual imbalanced decision problem.
