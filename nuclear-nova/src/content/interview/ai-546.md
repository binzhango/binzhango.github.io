## Question

The patient's chart already lists their medications. How would you use that to improve transcription of drug names, and how would you keep it from backfiring?

## Brief answer

Use the medication list as a soft recognition hint with explicit provenance, not as proof that a drug was spoken or is currently taken. Keep acoustic evidence, chart history, and clinician-confirmed facts distinct. Context can improve recognition of rare names but can also cause the system to hallucinate a familiar medication over ambiguous audio.

## Detailed answer

**Suggested approach.** Supply a bounded pronunciation vocabulary or contextual bias list to ASR and retain alternative hypotheses and confidence. Validate patient and encounter identity before using chart context. When the sound is ambiguous, show the candidate and source or request clarification rather than silently filling it from history.

Evaluate medication-name accuracy, false insertions, dose/unit errors, and negation on cases with stale and deliberately misleading chart lists. Compare audio-only and context-assisted systems using blinded review. Tag a chart-derived fact as chart-derived in the note, with its date, instead of attributing it to the patient. Reconcile discontinued medications and similar-sounding names through the clinical review workflow.

### Test whether context helps the acoustics or overrides them

Construct three matched evaluation groups: the correct medication appears in the chart, a similar-sounding incorrect medication appears, and the chart omits the spoken medication. Run the same audio with and without chart hints. The desired improvement is more correct recognition in the first group without an unacceptable rise in false substitutions or insertions in the other two.

Bound the bias vocabulary and include only context relevant to the encounter. A huge historical list can overwhelm the acoustic signal, and an outdated medication list may actively mislead recognition. Preserve candidate alternatives when the evidence is weak so the review interface can expose ambiguity instead of presenting a polished but unsupported certainty.

Keep provenance at the fact level. “Listed in the chart last month” and “patient reports taking today” should remain separate records until reconciled. Even perfect recognition of a drug name does not establish adherence, dose, route, or current use.

For an ambiguous name, present the uncertain transcript span and relevant chart candidates to the authorized clinician. Do not silently insert a full regimen from the chart. Measure critical medication errors separately from general word error rate, because a transcript can improve overall while becoming worse on the small set of words that most affect the resulting clinical document.
